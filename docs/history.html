Historical morphology: from PIE to Old Irish

A companion to the first 28 days of Stifter's Sengoídelc (2006). This is a comparative guide, not a substitute for a critical grammar or a claim that every reconstructed stage is attested.

The five questions behind every paradigm

When an Old Irish inflection looks unlike Latin, Ancient Greek or Sanskrit, separate five different historical processes:

  1. Inheritance. Which category or morphological opposition existed in Proto-Indo-European (PIE)? A shared nominative–accusative neuter form is an example.
  2. Phonological development. Which vowels or consonants of old endings were affected by Celtic/Irish sound changes? Old Irish root-vowel raising, palatalization and loss of unstressed syllables often encode these changes.
  3. Morphologization. Did an originally phonetic effect become a grammatical marker? Mutation on a following adjective can preserve a case distinction that disappeared on the noun itself.
  4. Analogy or morphological replacement. Did a formation from one paradigm replace an inherited form in another? The thematic genitive singular -ī and the innovative long neuter plural are key examples.
  5. Grammaticalization. Did an independent word or clitic become an affix-like marker or an obligatory grammatical construction? The definite article and the absolute/conjunct verb split illustrate related kinds of restructuring.

Notation: an asterisk * marks a reconstructed, not attested, linguistic form. The subscript in h₂ is a modern PIE reconstruction convention. A form cited for Proto-Celtic is not something documented in an Ogham inscription. “Inherited” can refer to a grammatical category even when Old Irish has innovated its overt marker.

1. Thematic nouns: ending loss is not case loss

A standard reconstructed PIE thematic masculine singular contrasts nominative -os and accusative -om. In Latin the contrast remains overt (equus/equum); Old Irish ech/ech has identical bare forms. Yet the accusative grammatical environment can still nasalize a following word.

The distinction between phonetic inheritance and morphological innovation matters. Two Old Irish case forms can become homophonous, or conversely an old suffix may disappear while leaving consonantal or vowel-quality cues.

A particularly revealing Italo-Celtic problem

Latin -ī (genitive singular, e.g. virī) and Celtic -ī (attested in Ogham Irish MAQI/MAQQI, ‘of the son’) were once widely interpreted as a common Proto-Italo-Celtic innovation. The more recent analysis by Weiss argues that the actual substitution of older thematic -osyo occurred separately in historical Celtic and Italic branches. It may be more useful to ask whether they shared an earlier morphological resource that was independently pressed into genitive service, rather than to assume a single completed innovation inherited by both.

This is a good example of an apparently shared ending whose historical date and status remain contested, not a reason to abandon comparison.

2. Vowel affection and morphological reanalysis

Inflected forms such as fer/fir/fiur, cenn/cinn/ciunn or macc/maicc show how a following vowel could change a preceding stressed vowel or consonant before that following vowel disappeared. Such changes can eventually become more reliable case markers than the original endings.

A schematic pathway is:

older stem + case suffix → vowel harmony/palatalization → weakening or loss of suffix → stem alternation learned as an inflectional form.

This is not simply the PIE e/o ablaut system preserved unchanged. It is a new layer of Celtic/Irish stem allomorphy, sometimes interacting with inherited ablaut.

The Ogham spellings MAQI / MAQQI are valuable precisely because a final genitive -I is still written: they provide material evidence for an earlier stage preceding the full Old Irish reduction.

3. Neuter and feminine formations

PIE neuter nominative and accusative identity is strongly reconstructible and remains a structural feature of Old Irish cenn and cride paradigms. PIE neuter plurals often display the -eh₂ family of morphology. However, Old Irish's short neuter plural is the conservative continuation in its normal sound history, while a long variant such as cenna appears to have been extended analogically (compare adjectival and pronominal material). Its visible -a is therefore not automatic proof of direct phonetic preservation of an ancient ending.

The feminine -eh₂ stem-forming family also continues into Celtic -ā- and Old Irish a-stem nouns such as tuath. The -eh₂ family has possible historical links to collective formations, and its role in the emergence of the Indo-European feminine gender remains a major comparative problem. An inherited feminine stem class does not entail identical case endings in Latin rosa and Old Irish tuath.

4. Pronouns, articles and grammar across word boundaries

PIE had demonstratives, but there is no reason to project the full Old Irish definite article paradigm back into PIE. Celtic demonstrative material, especially sindo- in the Gaulish/Insular comparison, was eventually grammaticalized into article systems.

Old Irish in/ind/inna therefore represents a later, reduced determiner paradigm, still participating in gender/case agreement. Fusion with prepositions—do + article → don, i + article → isin—extends this process. Moreover, postnominal lenition/nasalization can carry information which an older case ending expressed by a segment inside the inflected word.

These facts demonstrate an important type of morphological innovation: grammatical information migrates from an overt ending to the structure of the entire noun phrase.

5. Weak conjugations: formation history versus personal endings

Two layers must be distinguished in the Old Irish present tense:

  • Stem formation: how the verb was derived. W1 has an -ā- historical pattern; W2a includes -eyé--related denominative formations; W2b has old causative/iterative roots with o-grade and an -éye- suffix.
  • Finite inflection: how the stem receives person/number and absolute/conjunct forms.

W2a *leicid*** and **W2b *roithid*** share finite W2 endings even though their older stem-building histories differ. A historical **derivational contrast may become a relatively opaque conjugation subclass; analogy can make the two subclasses inflect alike.

The absolute/conjunct distinction is not a direct synonym for PIE primary/secondary endings. On the influential Cowgill-particle account, an ancient second-position clitic, reconstructed by many as eti, affected word-final verbal material differently depending on whether a preverb was present. Over time these syntactically conditioned outcomes became two verbal ending series (caraid versus ní·cara). The exact clitic theory and the chronology are debated, although the presence of old clitic and early apocope effects is widely discussed.

When you find a reconstruction in a daily lesson, verify what the reconstruction actually refers to (root, stem, case ending, clitic or word). A shared morphological formation is not necessarily a shared lexical cognate, and a typological parallel is not automatically a genealogical one.
