02 / FOCUS

The rule, clearly

Here is a classic trap: caraid and ní·caraid have the same letters at the end, but different grammatical persons. The former is 3rd singular absolute; the latter is 2nd plural conjunct. Parsing starts with the left edge of the verb phrase.

A second useful reminder: the 2nd plural absolute ending has an assimilated spelling in glantae (compare regular carthae). The important thing is that both are 2nd plural absolute.

Two quick steps: (1) identify independent or dependent environment; (2) then assign person and number.

Sources: Stifter (2006), §8.4 and exercises in §§8.5, 9.2; all example glosses here are pedagogical.

04 / HISTORICAL MORPHOLOGY

PIE → Celtic → Old Irish

Extended analysis of inheritance, sound change, analogy and innovations · optional 5–8 minutes

Historical analysis and sources (tap to collapse)

1. Historical source of the two ending series

PIE distinguished finite subject agreement, but Old Irish developed a further division between absolute and conjunct inflection. The two-series system is a hallmark of Insular Celtic, not a transparent retention of a PIE present-versus-past contrast.

Grammatical environment Old Irish sample Why the series is chosen
simple independent verb caraid absolute
after negative ní ní·cara conjunct
independent 2pl carthae absolute
after ní, 2pl ní·caraid conjunct

2. The clitic hypothesis in historical steps

In a widely used model, an old second-position clitic stood after an initial verb but after a preverbal particle when such a particle began the clause. Its distribution affected which parts of the verbal complex retained final vowels or extra material. Later reductions created two characteristic endings, which became morphological alternatives selected by syntax.

This is a schematic derivation, not a demonstrated transcription of one specific prehistoric sentence. Proposed identities and forms of the clitic differ; subject-pronoun and other accounts have also been defended. The clitic analysis is especially useful because it explains why ní causes conjunct inflection without changing the tense.

3. What is inherited?

The person/number distinctions are ancient; the conditioning rule and double set of endings are innovations. Avoid treating absolute = PIE primary and conjunct = PIE secondary as established sound-law equations.

References: Stifter (2006), §§8.3–8.5; Griffith (2020), “The Cowgill particle”; Le Mair, Secondary Verbs in Old Irish.
