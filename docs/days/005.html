The short plural is particularly common after an article or numeral: inna cenn ‘the heads’. The long plural is useful when plural number must be signaled by the noun itself.

02 / FOCUS

One point

Neuter nominative and accusative remain identical in the plural, but Old Irish offers two plural realizations. The short form resembles the singular in bare spelling (cenn); the long form adds -a (cenna).

This is not a nominative-versus-accusative contrast: both forms can serve both cases. The choice is partly influenced by the surrounding noun phrase; the short form commonly follows a word already indicating plural number.

Use the ending -aib to spot the prepositional plural cennaib quickly.

Source: Stifter (2006), §6.6; compare Thurneysen, neuter o-stem flexion.

04 / HISTORICAL MORPHOLOGY

PIE → Celtic → Old Irish

Historical analysis and sources (tap to collapse)

1. The inherited neuter plural

PIE neuter nominative and accusative plurals generally had one shared form, often reconstructed for thematic stems with the collective/plural element -eh₂. Celtic and Old Irish retain nom.–acc. identity, but their surface forms can be unexpectedly short.

Stifter's important historical point is that the Old Irish short plural (cenn) is the form continuing the Celtic/Indo-European preforms. Its older suffix has been reduced, leaving no extra visible syllable; lenition of a following word is one of its grammatical effects.

2. So what is long cenna?

Form Function Historical interpretation
cenn (short) neuter nom./acc. plural conservative continuation after ending loss
cenna (long) the same cases innovative analogical expansion in Old Irish, probably associated with adjective/pronominal patterns
cennaib prepositional plural inherited Celtic oblique plural morphology in a remodeled form

The long plural becomes useful when no article or numeral already supplies plural number. After an article or numeral, the short plural is particularly natural.

3. A morphological innovation, not a new case

The short/long opposition is not a PIE nominative/accusative opposition. It is a new distribution between two realizations of the same neuter case form. This distinction is crucial: a visible -a in cenna is not proof that the longer Old Irish variant is the direct, unmodified reflex of the PIE ending. Analogy can restore syllabic material in a paradigm after sound change has removed an older ending.

References: Stifter (2006), §6.6 (explicitly treats short plural as the older continuation); Thurneysen, A Grammar of Old Irish, neuter plural.
