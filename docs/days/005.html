L / N / H / 0 - how to read the following-word markers

Following-word effects: L lenition; N nasalization; H historical aspiration/gemination; 0 no regular L/N/H effect prescribed here. The marker belongs to the form before it and describes the next closely connected word, not a suffix. Effects need not be visible in spelling; phonological and syntactic restrictions still apply. Prepositional = the case often called dative. The vocative particle a itself lenites the noun; a marker at the end of the vocative phrase describes what follows the noun.

02 / FOCUS

The rule, clearly

Short and long neuter plurals

The neuter noun cenn ‘head’ has two nominative/accusative plural forms:

  • Short plural: cenn. Its written form is identical to the singular, but it is plural in context. It is especially common after an article or numeral that already indicates plurality. In this paradigm, it has an L effect (lenition) on a following word.
  • Long plural: cenna. The additional -a makes the plural visible on the noun itself. It is especially useful where no preceding word already indicates plurality. The table marks its default following-word effect H, while lenition also occurs in some contexts.

Both variants serve both the nominative and accusative plural. Their difference is therefore short versus long plural, not subject case versus object case. The distribution is context-sensitive rather than an absolute rule: the long form is not forbidden after an article, nor is the short form limited to those contexts.

The short form continues the older plural pattern after final-syllable reduction; the long form is a later analogical expansion. The two types should be learned as alternatives within the same neuter paradigm.

Source: Stifter (2006), §6.6; compare Thurneysen, neuter o-stem flexion.

04 / HISTORICAL MORPHOLOGY

PIE → Celtic → Old Irish

Extended analysis of inheritance, sound change, analogy and innovations · optional 5–8 minutes

Historical analysis and sources (tap to collapse)

1. The inherited neuter plural

PIE neuter nominative and accusative plurals generally had one shared form, often reconstructed for thematic stems with the collective/plural element -eh₂. Celtic and Old Irish retain nom.–acc. identity, but their surface forms can be unexpectedly short.

Stifter's important historical point is that the Old Irish short plural (cenn) is the form continuing the Celtic/Indo-European preforms. Its older suffix has been reduced, leaving no extra visible syllable; lenition of a following word is one of its grammatical effects.

The long plural becomes useful when no article or numeral already supplies plural number. After an article or numeral, the short plural is particularly natural.

3. A morphological innovation, not a new case

The short/long opposition is not a PIE nominative/accusative opposition. It is a new distribution between two realizations of the same neuter case form. This distinction is crucial: a visible -a in cenna is not proof that the longer Old Irish variant is the direct, unmodified reflex of the PIE ending. Analogy can restore syllabic material in a paradigm after sound change has removed an older ending.

References: Stifter (2006), §6.6 (explicitly treats short plural as the older continuation); Thurneysen, A Grammar of Old Irish, neuter plural.
