Following-word effects: L lenition; N nasalization; H historical aspiration/gemination; 0 no regular L/N/H effect prescribed here. The marker belongs to the form before it and describes the next closely connected word, not a suffix. Effects need not be visible in spelling; phonological and syntactic restrictions still apply. 02 / FOCUS

The rule, clearly

Read the forms: function → change

PIE -eh₂- became Celtic -ā- in this stem class; the a-stem label describes historical morphology, not a visible final vowel in Old Irish tuath. Latin rosa illustrates the class, not a cognate of tuath.

The term feminine a-stem names a historical category, not a promise that every Old Irish case form will end in -a. In Celtic prehistory a stem-final long -ā- reflects an older sequence conventionally written PIE -eh₂ (the numeral marks a reconstructed laryngeal).

Later Irish changes obscured that old thematic vowel; the case system nevertheless survives as vowel quality changes and endings such as tuaithe (genitive singular) or tuatha (plural).

For comparison, Latin often preserves a more transparent final vowel, as in rosa. Focus on structural inheritance rather than making Latin a parent language of Irish.

Sources: Stifter (2006), §§6.1, 10.1–10.2; Thurneysen, A Grammar of Old Irish, ā-stem nouns; standard Celtic/IE historical morphology.

1. Reconstructing the -eh₂ stem class

The feminine a-stem class in Old Irish ultimately belongs to the PIE -eh₂ morphological family. In the Celtic branch, the laryngeal h₂ was lost but influenced the preceding vowel, yielding a long -ā- stem-forming element at an intermediate stage.

1. Reconstructing the -eh₂ stem class
Historical layer Stem class Illustration
PIE -eh₂ feminine/collective morphology reconstructed suffix, not an attested word

Old Irish a-stems with various surface endings tuath, gen. tuaithe, pl. tuatha
Latin first-declension -a- stems rosa, pl. rosae

Important: The lexical words tuath and rosa are not cognates; the suffixal morphological class is the comparison.

2. Was -eh₂ originally feminine, or collective?

A major historical question is the relationship between neuter collective morphology and the rise of a distinct feminine gender. The -eh₂ family appears in both contexts in comparative reconstruction; some accounts connect the emergence of feminine -eh₂-stems with an earlier collective pattern that was reinterpreted. The precise chronology is debated, and one should not assert that a single suffix “created all feminine nouns.”

Evidence from Anatolian, where the oldest gender system differs from the familiar masculine/feminine/neuter tripartition, is one motivation for reconstructing earlier stages with fewer gender categories. This remains an historical hypothesis to evaluate against broader IE evidence.

3. Celtic innovations after the inherited stem class

In Old Irish, syncope, final-vowel loss, palatalization and analogy made a historical -ā- class appear as tuath/tuaith/tuaithe/tuatha. The stem-class ancestry is old, but the visible case and plural pattern is not a direct PIE paradigm.

References: Stifter (2006), §§10–11; Thurneysen on ā-stems; compare standard reconstructions of PIE feminine/collective -eh₂ morphology.
