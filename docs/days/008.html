02 / FOCUS

One point

The masculine article has a basic singular form in, but the accusative's grammatical effect differs from the nominative's even when the written sequence looks the same. A phrase such as in fer may therefore require context to disambiguate.

The genitive and prepositional are linked with lenition. Article variants before vowel-like sounds and f/l/r/n often show ind or dond (as in ind fir, dond fiur). For today's practice, recognize the broad pattern; you do not have to derive every variant.

Read together: in fer ‘the man’ (subject), ind fir ‘of the man’, dond fiur ‘for the man’.

Source: Stifter (2006), §7.1; detailed variant conditioning in §7.2.

04 / HISTORICAL MORPHOLOGY

PIE → Celtic → Old Irish

Extended analysis of inheritance, sound change, analogy and innovations · optional 5–8 minutes

Historical analysis and sources (tap to collapse)

1. A definite article is not an inherited PIE case suffix

PIE had an elaborate system of demonstrative pronouns (including so- / to- stems), but no single article paradigm that all daughter languages inherited. Old Irish in, therefore, is not the direct continuation of a dedicated “PIE definite article.” Celtic demonstratives—including a form usually reconstructed as sindo-—were grammaticalized into the definite article of Insular Celtic.

2. What was inherited from the demonstrative?

Demonstratives already inflected for gender, case and number. After a demonstrative became a regular determiner (‘that particular man’ → ‘the man’), it could retain traces of that older agreement. But the surface article was shortened and fused with neighboring words.

Level Morphology Result
PIE deictic pronoun with gender/case forms no obligatory the

3. The new location of grammatical information

In in fer (nom.) versus ind fir (gen.), article shape, noun inflection and mutation work together. This is an innovation of the noun phrase, not simply loss of inflection. Compare the independent development of a definite article from demonstratives in Greek (ho) and in the Romance languages: their history is typologically similar, but not evidence of one shared post-PIE article innovation.

References: Stifter (2006), §7.1; Luján, “From demonstratives to articles in the Celtic languages”; Thurneysen, A Grammar of Old Irish, article and demonstratives.
