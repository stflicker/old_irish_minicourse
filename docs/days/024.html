02 / FOCUS

One point

The contrast between absolute and conjunct forms is not simply positive versus negative. Ní triggers conjunct forms, but other preverbal particles do so as well. The grammatical trigger is how the verb is placed in the clause, not the affirmative/negative meaning.

Historically this double system emerged within Celtic. Its detailed origins are disputed; a common influential explanation appeals to old second-position particles and the phonological reshaping of the verbal complex. Treat this as a comparative hypothesis, not a directly attested Proto-Indo-European absolute-versus-conjunct opposition.

Today's goal is one clean contrast: caraid versus ní·cara.

Source: Stifter (2006), §12.3; cf. McCone, Towards a Relative Chronology of Ancient and Medieval Celtic Sound Change and discussions of the absolute/conjunct system.

1. The problem: why two endings for one grammatical function?

Compare caraid ‘loves’ with ní·cara ‘does not love’. Both are present indicative 3sg. PIE had person/number endings—commonly compare -ti for a third-person present—but no directly attested equivalent of the Old Irish absolute/conjunct split selected by the presence of ní.

2. The influential “Cowgill particle” analysis

A schematic historical explanation is:

Early word-order context Proposed clitic position Old Irish outcome
verb + clitic at the start of the clause particle after the verb absolute morphology
preverb/negative + clitic + verb particle before the verb conjunct morphology

The clitic's placement could protect some final verbal material in one context while letting it be lost or remodeled in the other. Ordinary second-position clitic behavior thereby became a formal opposition in the conjugation. A frequent proposal identifies the particle with a form related to eti, but its exact shape and ultimate origin remain debated.

3. Other approaches, and why this is an innovation

Earlier scholarship attempted to derive the two sets from PIE primary vs. secondary endings or from postverbal subject clitics; variants of these analyses remain part of the scholarly discussion. A difficulty with a simple primary/secondary equation is that PIE primarily used those ending series in a tense/aspect distribution, unlike the Old Irish syntactic distribution. The now widespread particle-based account treats both Old Irish sets as deriving from inherited finite verb morphology reorganized by Celtic prosody and syntax.

Result: The person/number categories are inherited, but their division into absolute and conjunct conjugations is a major Insular Celtic innovation.

References: Stifter (2006), §12.3; Griffith (2020), “The Cowgill particle”; “Deriving the Old Irish Clause”; Le Mair, Secondary Verbs in Old Irish.
