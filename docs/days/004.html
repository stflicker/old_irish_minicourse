L / N / H / 0 - how to read the following-word markers

Following-word effects: L lenition; N nasalization; H historical aspiration/gemination; 0 no regular L/N/H effect prescribed here. The marker belongs to the form before it and describes the next closely connected word, not a suffix. Effects need not be visible in spelling; phonological and syntactic restrictions still apply. Prepositional = the case often called dative. The vocative particle a itself lenites the noun; a marker at the end of the vocative phrase describes what follows the noun.

02 / FOCUS

The rule, clearly

Read the forms: function → change

Form: neuter cenn ‘head’. Function: singular subject and object both use cenn. Change: genitive cinn and prepositional ciunn have different stem vowels. Takeaway: first distinguish case functions, then observe the vowel forms.

The most useful neuter rule is nominative = accusative, across numbers. With cenn, both singular forms are cenn and both carry an N effect on what follows. By contrast, masculine fer has a non-nasalizing nominative versus nasalizing accusative.

Other singular cases follow familiar o-stem patterns: cinn (genitive) and ciunn (prepositional).

Take a moment to parse a cenn as a neuter noun phrase in the nominative or accusative, rather than assuming every a means a possessive.

Source: Stifter (2006), §6.6, neuter o-stems.

04 / HISTORICAL MORPHOLOGY

PIE → Celtic → Old Irish

Historical analysis and sources (tap to collapse)

1. PIE neuter case identity is an inheritance

The neuter nominative and accusative singular were formally identical in the PIE thematic declension, with an ending conventionally reconstructed -om. Compare Latin bellum and Sanskrit yugám, each serving as nominative or accusative neuter singular. The Old Irish pair cenn (nom.) : cenn (acc.) continues this structural identity.

The sound changes responsible for cenn/cinn/ciunn make the paradigm appear to change its root, but these shapes are not separate roots.

3. Innovation without abandoning the old case system

The neuter nom.–acc. identity is old. By contrast, the loss of overt PIE case endings, the visible internal alternations they left behind, and the phrase-level grammatical mutation pattern are specifically Celtic/Irish developments. The definite article a in neuter nominative/accusative noun phrases is a separate, later grammaticalized determiner; PIE did not have the Old Irish article paradigm.
