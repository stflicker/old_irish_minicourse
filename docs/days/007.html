02 / FOCUS

One point

Masculine thematic (o-stem) nouns were widespread in early Indo-European. Compare the endings rather than forcing one-to-one spellings: Latin distinguishes equus/equum in the singular; Old Irish ech/ech has lost that visible distinction, although syntactic mutation can still signal a case contrast.

In the plural, Old Irish eich/echu distinguishes nominative and accusative directly again. The loss of final syllables and compensating grammatical cues makes early Irish especially interesting for comparative morphology.

Practice: cover the Old Irish column and reconstruct the four forms from memory.

References: Stifter (2006), §§6.4–6.6; Thurneysen, A Grammar of Old Irish, o-stems; standard comparative Indo-European thematic paradigms.

1. Reconstruct the architecture, not only look-alike words

PIE thematic nouns carried a stem vowel -o- and distinct endings; their descendant paradigms may preserve, replace, or hide those endings.

Function PIE / early IE comparison Later Celtic/Old Irish development Type of historical change
Masc. nom. sg. -os ech, no final written ending regular sound change
Masc. acc. sg. -om ech plus N environment sound change + retained sandhi
Masc. gen. sg. PIE -osyo; Celtic -ī eich morphological replacement, then sound change
Masc. nom. pl. -oi spread from pronouns eich analogical extension
Masc. acc. pl. older -ons echu sound change
Neuter nom./acc. one shared form in both numbers cenn, cenna (pl. variants) inheritance + later analogy

The forms are comparative shorthand: actual intermediary stages depend on the lexical stem and sound laws.

2. Why Latin is not the 'missing' Irish stage

Latin equus/equum/equī/equōs is useful because it often preserves visible vowel/consonant sequences. But Latin is a sister branch, not an intermediate between PIE and Old Irish. Likewise, Sanskrit may preserve a category while innovating its own endings.

3. A debated Italo-Celtic parallel

Latin and Celtic both developed a thematic genitive singular *-ī, conspicuously unlike the inherited PIE ***-osyo. Older discussions saw this as a strong “Italo-Celtic” common innovation. Recent comparative analysis argues that **the replacement may have happened independently in individual Italic and Celtic varieties. Thus the correct historical statement is formal convergence with disputed chronology, not a guaranteed shared innovation.

References: Stifter (2006), §§6.4–6.6; Thurneysen, A Grammar of Old Irish; Cambridge, “Italo-Celtic” (2022).
