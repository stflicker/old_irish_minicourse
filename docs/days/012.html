1. One verb form: its parts

Reading order: first particle or preverb · (infixed pronoun) · root and stem vowel + ending. The stress falls right after the raised dot, which is the course’s way of marking the boundary (some books write a hyphen or a colon).

1. One verb form: its parts
Part What it does Example
First particle or preverb Puts the verb in dependent position; carries the meaning of a preverb or a particle such as ní ‘not’, in ‘?’ ní·cara ‘does not love’; do·biur ‘I give’
Infixed pronoun A pronoun inside the verb: the object (‘him, her, them’) Lessons 25, 28, 38
Augment (ro and others) Makes the verb perfective (‘has said’) or potential (‘can say’) as·bert ‘said’ → as·rubart ‘has said’
Root and stem vowel The meaning of the verb and its class ní·car-a
Ending Person, number, active or deponent or passive, and the series ní·caraid (2nd pl.)

There are no subject pronouns: the ending alone gives the person (§8.3). A verb has no infinitive; the dictionary form is the independent 3rd singular present (§8.2).

2. Person and number, with the two series

The present indicative of caraid ‘loves’ (W1). Absolute endings are used with nothing before the verb; conjunct endings after any particle or preverb (here ní).

Imperative commands, to all six persons present stem 2nd sg. is the bare stem; 3rd sg. has its own ending; the other persons equal the conjunct present L. 37

The secondary endings belong to exactly three categories: imperfect, conditional, past subjunctive. They have no absolute forms; a simple verb in independent position takes the empty particle no·. The imperative has no absolute forms either.

6. The verb classes

Every tense is built on a stem, and the stem depends on the class (§8.2, McCone’s classification). Decide by the 3rd singular conjunct.

6. The verb classes
Class Model verb Absolute Conjunct after ní Short description
W1 caraid ‘loves’ caraid ní·cara ends in -a; root-final consonant not palatalized
W2 leicid ‘leaves, allows’ leicid ní·leici ends in -i; root-final consonant palatalized
S1 beirid ‘carries’ beirid ní·beir ends in a consonant; palatalization alternates
S2 gaibid ‘takes’ gaibid ní·gaib ends in a consonant; root-final consonant palatalized
S3 crenaid ‘buys’ crenaid ní·cren ends in a consonant; the n is only in the present stem
H1–H3 hiatus verbs the root ends in a vowel

Palatalized means pronounced with the tongue raised towards the hard palate; older grammars call it “slender”. W stands for weak, S for strong, H for hiatus.

02 / FOCUS

The rule, clearly

Three questions place every verb form

1. Which class? The 3rd singular conjunct decides: a final vowel means a weak verb (W1 -a, W2 -i); a final consonant means a strong verb (S1–S3). Hiatus verbs have a vowel at the end of the root.

2. Which series? A simple verb with nothing before it is independent and takes absolute endings. Any particle or preverb in front makes it dependent, with conjunct endings. Compound verbs have only conjunct endings; independent and dependent are then told apart by the stress (Days 31–32).

3. Which tense, mood and voice? Read the stem and the ending together. The three categories with secondary endings are the imperfect, conditional and past subjunctive; all the others have their own endings.

Quick answers

  • Why do I meet three classification systems? Stifter follows McCone (W, S, H); Thurneysen’s Grammar of Old Irish uses A and B classes; Strachan has a third. Stifter’s Appendix F.6 gives a conversion table.
  • Why two series of endings? They are not the PIE primary and secondary endings (Stifter §12.3, note 6); see Day 24.
  • Does every class have every tense? Yes in principle, but each tense has its own stem formations, which depend on the class (Appendix F.8–F.10).
  • What is “secondary”? Stifter’s name for the endings of the imperfect, conditional and past subjunctive (§49.1).

Historical development: where do the present classes come from?

Historical development: where do the present classes come from?
Class Source (Stifter) Example
W1 mostly denominative (made from nouns and adjectives); many Latin loans marb ‘dead’ → marbaid ‘kills’; scríbaid < Latin scrībere
W2a mostly denominative, from o- and ā-stem nouns scél ‘story’ → do·scéulai; rím → rímid
W2b old causative and iterative verbs, root vowel *o reithid ‘runs’ → roithid ‘makes run’
S1 old thematic *-e/o-: *bʰereti, *bʰeronti > Proto-Celtic *bereti, *beronti beirid, berait
S2 old thematic *-i̯e/o-: *gʷʰedʰi̯eti, *gʷʰedʰi̯onti > Proto-Celtic *gʷedi̯eti, *gʷedi̯onti (Gaulish uediiumi ‘I pray’) guidid, guidit
S3 old nasal-infix presents of roots ending in a laryngeal; Proto-Celtic *kʷrinati, *kʷrinanti (Gaulish prinas, Old Welsh prinit) crenaid, crenait
  1. Weak classes are younger. W1 and W2 are mainly built inside Irish or Celtic from nouns and adjectives; the strong classes continue inherited PIE present types.
  2. The palatalization pattern has a cause. In S1 the thematic vowel was *o where the root consonant is non-palatalized and *e where it is palatalized (Stifter, §18.3, note 3).
  3. Absolute and conjunct are a Celtic development, not a PIE contrast (Day 24).

Summary. The classification and the sources are Stifter’s. Stifter states that his reconstructions reflect his own opinion; for the S2 and S3 prehistory he calls the intermediate Insular Celtic stages “far from being undisputed” (§21.6). The tense, mood and voice tables condense the opening sections of Stifter’s later lessons (listed under Sources); the details of each category are taught when we reach it. The PIE input of S3 and of the hiatus verbs is not given here.
