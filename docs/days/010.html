L / N / H / 0 - how to read the following-word markers

Following-word effects: L lenition; N nasalization; H historical aspiration/gemination; 0 no regular L/N/H effect prescribed here. The marker belongs to the form before it and describes the next closely connected word, not a suffix. Effects need not be visible in spelling; phonological and syntactic restrictions still apply. Prepositional = the case often called dative. The vocative particle a itself lenites the noun; a marker at the end of the vocative phrase describes what follows the noun.

Short neuter plurals carry L; the long -a plural is shown with the course default H. Lenition after -a is also found: this is a documented variation, not a universal ban on lenition.

A preceding nasalizing article can produce a ndliged. That is an incoming effect on dliged, separate from the outgoing marker shown here. Long -a plurals can also be followed by lenition.

Worked contrast: a N + dliged gives a ndliged. The neuter noun dliged N then has its own outgoing effect in the nominative/accusative singular. The same article before cenn does not normally write an extra n: a cenn.

02 / FOCUS

The rule, clearly

Read the forms: function → change

a cenn is neuter ‘the head’ as subject or object. In a ndliged, nasalization makes the article's grammatical effect visible.

The standout neuter form is a in both nominative and accusative singular. This exactly matches the neuter noun's nominative–accusative syncretism.

An example with a visible nasal marker is a ndliged ‘the law’. With cenn, Stifter writes a cenn; an N effect is nonetheless assigned to the phrase, even if not separately spelled.

All the other article cases largely follow the masculine pattern. In the plural, inna also covers nominative and accusative.

Source: Stifter (2006), §7.3 and the two paradigms preceding §7.4.

1. Two histories meet in the neuter noun phrase

The Old Irish neuter pattern combines an inherited PIE category with an innovative Insular Celtic article. PIE thematic neuters had nominative = accusative (e.g. a single reconstructed -om singular ending); no dedicated definite article of the Old Irish type is reconstructible for PIE.

The neuter article a should not be equated mechanically with a supposed PIE “neuter a-ending.” It belongs to a later determiner paradigm. The noun's own case/number inflection has a different lineage.

3. Conservatism and innovation together

It is possible for the same category to be conservative while the way it is expressed is novel: Old Irish preserves neuter nom.–acc. identity but often relies on an article, sound-conditioned consonant quality, and mutation rather than transparent final case syllables. This is why checking the complete phrase is essential.
