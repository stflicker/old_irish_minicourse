mo, do, a — and why mutation matters

01 / FORMS

Forms and patterns

Possessor Possessive Example noun phrase
my mo (L) mo chenn ‘my head’
your (sg.) do (L) do chenn ‘your head’
his a (L) a chenn ‘his head’
her a (H) a cenn ‘her head’
their a (N) a cenn (nasalization not visible before all consonants)

L = lenition, N = nasalization, H = no lenition/nasalization with the appropriate historical aspiration effect. For a alone, the mutation pattern often provides the grammatical information.

02 / FOCUS

The rule, clearly

The most memorable rule is mo/do/a for my/your/his, but an important ambiguity appears: a also means her or their. The phonological effect on the possessed noun differentiates them: his a normally lenites, her a does not, and their a nasalizes.

Before a suitable initial consonant these distinctions are easy to see. Compare the instructive pair a chenn ‘his head’ versus a cenn ‘her head’. In contexts where nasalization is invisible, the surrounding sentence resolves ambiguity.

The pronouns agree with the possessor, not with the noun's gender. The same possessive word can modify a masculine, feminine or neuter noun.

Source: Stifter (2006), §12.4.

04 / HISTORICAL MORPHOLOGY

PIE → Celtic → Old Irish

Extended analysis of inheritance, sound change, analogy and innovations · optional 5–8 minutes

Historical analysis and sources (tap to collapse)

1. Possession is older than the Irish forms

PIE had personal pronouns with oblique/genitival uses and a rich set of enclitics and unstressed pronominal forms. Possessive determiners can historically develop from such genitival pronouns. Old Irish mo ‘my’, do ‘your’, and a ‘his/her/their’ form a reduced, preposed possessive series; it is not safe to identify each Irish vowel with one specific PIE case ending.

2. A striking morphological redistribution

Old Irish Possessor Following-word effect
mo 1sg L
do 2sg L
a 3sg masculine/neuter L
a 3sg feminine H
a 3pl N

These markers do not inflect for the gender of the noun possessed. The three meanings of a are distinguished in many contexts primarily by the mutation environment, a feature no longer interpretable as three different full pronominal endings.

3. What has been innovated?

First, old pronominal genitive functions were reanalyzed as unstressed possessive determiners. Second, the phonological consequences of earlier word boundaries and endings were grammaticalized into productive mutation classes. Their morphological function now exceeds the original phonetic conditioning.

This is analogous in broad type to an Indo-European possessive construction being reduced into a clitic-plus-noun phrase, but Old Irish's specific L/H/N three-way contrast is an Insular Celtic result, not an inherited PIE mutation paradigm.

References: Stifter (2006), §12.4; University of Texas, Old Irish possessives, §15.3; Thurneysen on pronominal flexion.
