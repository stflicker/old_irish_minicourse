02 / FOCUS

The rule, clearly

Look at the ending vowel before you look at the root

In fer the root vowel changes with the case. Same mutations as ech, plus a second signal inside the word.

  • Before an a-coloured ending (nom., acc., gen. pl., dat. pl.): fer, feraib. This is Stifter's “lowering” {L}.
  • Before *-ī or *-ū (gen. sg., nom. pl., prep. sg., acc. pl.): fir, fiur, firu. This is his “raising” {R}.
  • The prepositional fiur also shows u-infection: *-ū left a u before the last consonant.

Conditions (Stifter §6.5): only stressed short vowels of monosyllables and some disyllables; never long vowels or diphthongs; raising needs a single voiced consonant after the vowel, “not all details of which have been worked out”. Which words alternate must be learned (ech, macc do not).

Quick answers

  • Is e or i the basic vowel? Stifter's chain has *i throughout, so fer is the lowered shape. His formalism describes the same forms from the other end; the paradigm is identical.
  • Why does ech not give *ich? The voiceless ch blocks raising (see Question 2).
  • Why f-? Initial *u̯- became f- in Irish (compare find ‘white’ < *u̯indo-, Welsh gwyn).
  • Does son work the same way? Yes: o before *a, u before *ī/*ū.

Forms from Stifter p. 45. For Proto-Celtic *u̯iros Stifter cites an attested spelling, <uiros> (Gaulish).

  1. Vowel height follows the next vowel. In Primitive Irish the root vowel *i was lowered to e before *a (*u̯irah > fer) and stayed i before *ī and *ū (fir, firu). This is the a-affection / i- and u-affection of Irish handbooks.
  2. The endings are lost, the effect stays. After the final syllables fell, the root vowel was the only trace of the ending vowel, besides the mutation of the next word (vowel → L, *-ūh → H).
  3. u-infection. *-ū rounded the preceding consonant, giving fiur (and the optional euchu).
  4. Cognates. Latin vir, Sanskrit vīrá-, Gaulish uiros: the root vowel i is old; the e of fer is the Irish innovation.

Status. Regular: 1–3 (Stifter: “effects of historical developments, depending on the type of vowel that originally stood in the following syllable”). Replaced («): the genitive *-ī, as in ech.

Sources: Stifter (2006) §6.5, pp. 44–45 (incl. his note on McCone, Towards a Relative Chronology of Ancient and Medieval Celtic Sound Change, 1996, pp. 109–115); f- from *u̯-: find < *u̯indo- (search summary, not checked in a primary source); Primitive Irish affection terms: Wikipedia, “Phonological history of Old Irish” (secondary).
