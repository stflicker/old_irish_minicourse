L / N / H / 0 - how to read the following-word markers

Following-word effects: L lenition; N nasalization; H historical aspiration/gemination; 0 no regular L/N/H effect prescribed here. The marker belongs to the form before it and describes the next closely connected word, not a suffix. Effects need not be visible in spelling; phonological and syntactic restrictions still apply. Prepositional = the case often called dative. The vocative particle a itself lenites the noun; a marker at the end of the vocative phrase describes what follows the noun.

02 / FOCUS

The rule, clearly

Read the forms: function → change

tuath is nominative singular ‘tribe’; tuaithe is genitive singular ‘of the tribe’. The H marker tells you about the next word, not a suffix.

The feminine a-stem class introduces a new singular pattern. For tuath, nominative tuath contrasts with accusative tuaith — compare the masculine o-stem fer where both were simply fer.

The form tuaith also serves as the prepositional singular, but its phrase-level effects differ from the accusative. The genitive is tuaithe, ending in -e after a palatalized consonant.

Instead of reciting all five forms, test the two most diagnostic ones: tuath versus tuaith.

Source: Stifter (2006), §10.1, standard feminine a-stem pattern.

04 / HISTORICAL MORPHOLOGY

PIE → Celtic → Old Irish

Extended analysis of inheritance, sound change, analogy and innovations · optional 5–8 minutes

Historical analysis and sources (tap to collapse)

1. The historical stem-forming suffix

Feminine Old Irish a-stems continue the Celtic -ā- class, which in turn is related to PIE feminine -eh₂-. The label a-stem is therefore etymological: Old Irish tuath need not end in -a.

1. The historical stem-forming suffix
PIE morphological category Typical reconstructed suffix Old Irish outcome (illustrated)
Feminine nominative sg. stem in -eh₂ tuath
Feminine accusative sg. -eh₂-m tuaith, with nasalizing environment
Feminine genitive sg. -eh₂-s (one major pattern) tuaithe

The Old Irish forms are not obtained by simply deleting letters from these generic endings; Celtic sound changes and subsequent analogy operated on whole word forms.

2. Why did an a-stem acquire several stem shapes?

Older unstressed long vowels and consonants affected nearby segments before they were reduced or lost. As a result, the same lexical stem can end in different consonant qualities, and the former accusative nasal can still help distinguish its syntactic environment.

3. Inherited morphology versus innovation

A dedicated feminine stem-forming pattern has deep Indo-European roots, but the distribution of singular case forms in Old Irish is a product of later Celtic/Irish developments. Latin rosa, rosae, rosam use the same broad stem class, not the same sound changes. Old Irish tuath/tuaith/tuaithe is therefore an excellent exercise in separating inheritance of a category from innovation in its phonological expression.

References: Stifter (2006), §10.1; Thurneysen, A Grammar of Old Irish, feminine ā-stems; comparative -eh₂ morphology.
