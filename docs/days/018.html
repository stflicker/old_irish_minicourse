L / N / H / 0 - how to read the following-word markers

Following-word effects: L lenition; N nasalization; H historical aspiration/gemination; 0 no regular L/N/H effect prescribed here. The marker belongs to the form before it and describes the next closely connected word, not a suffix. Effects need not be visible in spelling; phonological and syntactic restrictions still apply. Prepositional = the case often called dative. The vocative particle a itself lenites the noun; a marker at the end of the vocative phrase describes what follows the noun.

02 / FOCUS

The rule, clearly

Read the forms: function → change

ind ingen ‘the girl’ contrasts with genitive inna ingine ‘of the girl’. Both article and noun inflect; ind is a vowel-conditioned article variant.

The feminine nominative takes an article with L effect, whereas the feminine accusative takes an N effect. The article itself often looks like in in both functions, so the noun phrase must be parsed as a whole.

The feminine genitive singular is very different: inna. This is a helpful contrast with masculine/neuter genitive singular in. In the plural the feminine article is also inna in nominative and accusative.

Try reading: ind ingen (nom.), inna ingine (gen.), in n-ingin (acc.).

Source: Stifter (2006), §§11.1–11.2; §11.4 for the complete ingen phrase paradigm.

1. Feminine articles are descendants of demonstratives

PIE demonstratives distinguished gender, number and case, providing a template for later definite article systems. Old Irish feminine in / inna descends from the Celtic demonstrative → article pathway, not from a PIE-specific feminine article in inna.

Some syncretic article shapes are old pronominal material reduced by phonology; others owe their distribution to analogical realignment. A bare inna can therefore have different gender/case functions.

3. Innovation in grammatical marking

Old Irish developed a case-sensitive mutation system inside noun phrases while conserving Indo-European agreement categories. The article itself is an innovation of later Celtic grammar; the noun's underlying feminine a-stem is older.

One comparative caution: Latin developed no definite article in its classical period, while Romance subsequently grammaticalized ille ‘that’. The pathway is analogous to Celtic, but the actual forms and dates are independent.

References: Stifter (2006), §§11.1–11.2; Luján, “From demonstratives to articles in the Celtic languages”; University of Texas Old Irish grammar.
