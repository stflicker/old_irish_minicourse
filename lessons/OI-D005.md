## Paradigm
| Plural case | Form | Key observation |
|---|---|---|
| Nominative = accusative (short) | **cenn** | L on a following word |
| Nominative = accusative (long) | **cenna** | ending **-a**, H effect |
| Genitive | **cenn** | N effect |
| Prepositional | **cennaib** | **-aib** |
| Vocative | **a chenna** | long-type form |

The short plural is particularly common after an article or numeral: *inna cenn* ‘the heads’. The long plural is useful when plural number must be signaled by the noun itself.
## One point
**Neuter nominative and accusative remain identical in the plural**, but Old Irish offers **two plural realizations**. The **short** form resembles the singular in bare spelling (*cenn*); the **long** form adds *-a* (*cenna*).

This is **not** a nominative-versus-accusative contrast: *both* forms can serve *both* cases. The choice is partly influenced by the surrounding noun phrase; the short form commonly follows a word already indicating plural number.

Use the ending **-aib** to spot the prepositional plural *cennaib* quickly.

*Source:* Stifter (2006), §6.6; compare Thurneysen, neuter *o*-stem flexion.
## Comparison (optional)
The old Indo-European neuter nominative–accusative plural commonly had an ending *-h₂*, reflected as *-a* in several branches. But the Old Irish short-versus-long **distribution is its own historical development**; do not map every Old Irish *-a* mechanically onto a single PIE case ending.
