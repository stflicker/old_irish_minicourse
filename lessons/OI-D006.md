## Paradigm

Following-word effects: [L] lenition · [N] nasalization · [H] aspiration / gemination · [0] none. They describe the *next* word, not the noun's own ending.

### *ech* ‘horse’ · masculine o-stem

| Case | Singular form + effect | Plural form + effect |
|---|---|---|
| Nominative | **ech** [0] | **eich** [L] |
| Genitive | **eich** [L] | **ech** [N] |
| Prepositional | **euch / eoch** [L] | **echaib** [0] |
| Accusative | **ech** [N] | **echu / euchu / eochu** [H] |
| Vocative | **a eich** [L] | **a echu / a euchu / a eochu** [H] |

## One point

### The next word separates the homonyms

| Spelling | Could be | The next word decides |
|---|---|---|
| ech, fer | nom. sg. [0] · acc. sg. [N] · gen. pl. [N] | unmutated → nominative; nasalized → accusative singular or genitive plural (sentence role separates those two) |
| eich | gen. sg. [L] · nom. pl. [L] | same effect: agreement and sentence role decide |
| fir | gen. sg. · nom. pl. · voc. sg. [L] | after *a* → vocative |
| echu, firu | acc. pl. · voc. pl. [H] | after *a* → vocative |
| cenn | nom./acc. sg. [N] · nom./acc. pl. [L] · gen. pl. [N] | nasalized → singular or genitive plural; lenited → plural |

Stifter's own phrases show the signal on the adjective: *int ech becc* (nom.), *ind eich bicc* (gen.), *in n-ech mbecc* (acc.).

**Quick answers**

- **Which words alternate at all?** Only stressed short vowels of short words, and only some of them. Stifter's note to §6.7: *corp*, *rosc*, *sorn* and *bedg* are raisable or lowerable; *ball* has u-infection; *macc* has none; the rest keep their vowel.
- **What about long vowels?** *dál*, *síl*, *slóg*, *múr*: no change, only palatalization (§6.8.1).
- **Is *é* regular?** No: *é* turns into a diphthong before palatal consonants or u-infection (*én* ‘bird’, nom. pl. *éoin*, prep. sg. *éun*; §6.8.1).
- **Self-test (Stifter §6.7):** decline *macc*, *dún* (n.), *corp*, *ball*, *roth*, *rosc* (n.), *sorn*, *bech*, *bedg*, *leth* (n.). Stifter gives no key; use the rules of Days 1–5.

## Comparison (optional)

### Historical card: why is a longer word not a copy of *fer*?

| Cell | Theoretical | Old Irish | What happened |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nom. sg. | — | **claideb** ‘sword’ | non-palatal final b |
| Gen. sg. | — | **claidib** | palatalized b (\*-ī) |
| Prep. sg. | — | **claidiub** | u-infection (\*-ū) |
| Acc. pl. | \*claidebu | **claidbiu** | syncope of *e*; cluster palatalized |
| Acc. pl. | \*carpatu | **cairptiu** (*carpat* ‘chariot’) | same |

Examples from Stifter §§6.8.4–6.8.5.

1. **Syncope.** “In pre-Old Irish times, after the loss of inherited final syllables, the vowel of every second, non-final syllable was deleted.” A three-syllable form such as \*claidebu loses the *e*.
2. **Quality of the cluster.** If the deleted vowel was historically front (e, ē, i, ī), the whole cluster is palatalized; if back (a, ā, o, ō, u, ū), non-palatalized. In *claideb* the lost vowel was “most probably \*ī”.
3. **Counter-rules.** A long vowel in the second syllable, especially from compensatory lengthening, blocks syncope: prep. pl. *cenelaib* (< Proto-Celtic \*kenetlom). Many analogical adjustments mean the rule often does not apply.
4. **Spelling.** The schwa is written *a* before a non-palatal and *i* before a palatal consonant: *claideb*, *claidib*; *salann*, *salainn*, *salunn*.

**Status.** Regular, with many exceptions; Stifter: “the one really has to know in each case which rules apply to a particular word”.

*Sources:* Stifter (2006) §§6.7–6.8, pp. 47–49. The relative chronology (apocope, then syncope, around the 6th century) is summarized in Wikipedia, “Primitive Irish” (secondary; McCone 1996 is the primary study).
