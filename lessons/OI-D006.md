## Paradigm
### Masculine *macc* ‘son’ — *o*-stem
| Case | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| Nominative | **macc** | **maicc** |
| Genitive | **maicc** | — |
| Accusative | **macc** | **maccu** |
| Prepositional | — | **maccaib** |

### Neuter *cenn* ‘head’ — *o*-stem
| Case | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| Nominative | **cenn** | **cenn / cenna** |
| Genitive | **cinn** | — |
| Accusative | **cenn** | **cenn / cenna** |
| Prepositional | — | **cennaib** |

**Read the contrast:** In the masculine plural, **maicc** (nominative) differs from **maccu** (accusative). In the neuter plural, **cenn** or **cenna** serves **both** cases. **—** means this lesson does not supply that form, not that Old Irish lacks it.

*These are pedagogical model forms, not a claim that every row is individually attested.*
## One point

### Read the forms: function → change

**Form:** masculine *maicc* versus *maccu*. **Function:** nominative plural versus accusative plural. **Contrast:** neuter *cenn / cenna* does not split those two cases. The separate tables make the gender difference visible without having to reconstruct it from prose.

Two five-second diagnostics. **(1)** If a masculine noun ends in a palatalized consonant in the nominative plural, its accusative plural can still be a different *-u* form (*maicc* versus *maccu*). **(2)** A neuter noun retains nominative–accusative identity and may have short and long plurals.

One useful variation: some masculine *o*-stems show **u-infection** inside the root, but *macc* does **not** insert such a *u* inside its stem. Its accusative plural **maccu** has a regular plural ending; that is a different phenomenon.

Try hiding the chart and recalling just the two genitive singulars.

*Source:* Stifter (2006), §§6.7–6.8 (exercise words and variation); §§6.4–6.6 for the paradigms.

## Comparison (optional)

### 1. The Ogham evidence matters

The comparison **macc : maicc** (‘son’, nominative/genitive singular) is not merely a classroom abstraction. Early Irish Ogham inscriptions preserve genitives written **MAQI / MAQQI**, before the extreme final-vowel reduction seen in classical Old Irish. These inscriptions make the earlier **genitive final *-i*** more tangible than the modern-looking alternation alone.

| Stage/evidence | Genitive singular ‘son’ | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| Early Ogham Irish | **MAQI / MAQQI** | still written with final **-I** |
| Old Irish | **maicc** | final vowel lost, palatal consonant preserved |
| Latin thematic nouns | e.g. **servī** | comparable *-ī* genitive, with a debated shared prehistory |

### 2. Multiple phonetic effects can encode one case

The genitive can be recognized by **palatalization** (*macc → maicc*) while an earlier accusative nasal can leave a **mutation effect** outside the noun. These two mechanisms have different phonological sources, despite now being taught together as case morphology.

### 3. A change in the system, not just its spelling

The shift from **visible final vowels to consonantal quality** is a major Irish innovation in *how* an inherited case paradigm is expressed. Meanwhile, the Celtic genitive in ***-ī*** is itself a **morphological replacement** relative to PIE thematic ***-osyo***. This distinction between **ending replacement** and **sound-driven loss** is central to comparative reconstruction.
