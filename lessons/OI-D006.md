## Paradigm

**Following-word effects:** [L] lenition; [N] nasalization; [H] historical aspiration/gemination; [0] no regular L/N/H effect prescribed here. The marker belongs to the form before it and describes the next closely connected word, not a suffix. Effects need not be visible in spelling; phonological and syntactic restrictions still apply. Prepositional = the case often called dative. The vocative particle a itself lenites the noun; a marker at the end of the vocative phrase describes what follows the noun.

## One point

### U-infection: the old ending leaves a trace

**The historical trigger.** An older **u-vowel in the ending** rounded neighbouring sounds. The o-stem dative ending developed through `*-ōi > *-ū > *-u`, then disappeared; its rounding could remain inside the stem: **`*wiru → fiur`**. These are schematic reconstructed stages, not manuscript spellings.

**The conditioning in macc.** Thurneysen (§170a) identifies **a + cc** as an environment that does not retain rounded consonant quality. Hence prepositional singular **macc**, alongside **cenn → ciunn**, where raising and rounding are visible.

**The same case in both genders.** Masculine **fiur** and neuter **ciunn** are both **dative/prepositional singular**. The gender difference concerns their plurals: masculine accusatives inherited **`*-ons > *-ūs > -u`**; neuter nominative–accusative plurals had an **a-type ending**, with no corresponding u-trigger.

**Why firu differs from fiur.** The accusative plural's earlier long **ū** survived as an ending, whereas the dative's shortened **u** disappeared. The stem glide is normally absent before the retained syllable in **firu** (§88). Stem **a** has additional developments, so this is not a universal rule against rounding before surviving **-u**.

> **The old ending and the stem's sound environment explain the pattern—not gender alone.**

For the two checks, locate genitive singular **maicc** and the two nominative/accusative plurals **cenn / cenna**, then hide their cells and recall them.

## Comparison (optional)

### 1. The Ogham evidence matters

The comparison **macc : maicc** (‘son’, nominative/genitive singular) is not merely a classroom abstraction. Early Irish Ogham inscriptions preserve genitives written **MAQI / MAQQI**, before the extreme final-vowel reduction seen in classical Old Irish. These inscriptions make the earlier **genitive final *-i*** more tangible than the modern-looking alternation alone.

| Stage/evidence | Genitive singular ‘son’ | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| Early Ogham Irish | **MAQI / MAQQI** | still written with final **-I** |
| Old Irish | **maicc** | final vowel lost, palatal consonant preserved |
| Latin thematic nouns | e.g. **servī** | comparable *-ī* genitive, with a debated shared prehistory |

### 2. Multiple phonetic effects can encode one case

The genitive can be recognized by **palatalization** (*macc → maicc*) while an earlier accusative nasal can leave a **mutation effect** outside the noun. These two mechanisms have different phonological sources, despite now being taught together as case morphology.

### 3. A change in the system, not just its spelling

The shift from **visible final vowels to consonantal quality** is a major Irish innovation in *how* an inherited case paradigm is expressed. Meanwhile, the Celtic genitive in ***-ī*** is itself a **morphological replacement** relative to PIE thematic ***-osyo***. This distinction between **ending replacement** and **sound-driven loss** is central to comparative reconstruction.
