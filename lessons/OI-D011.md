## Paradigm
| Target | Masculine phrase with **catt** ‘cat’ |
|---|---|
| Nominative sg. | **in catt bán** ‘the white cat’ |
| Genitive sg. | **in chaitt báin** ‘of the white cat’ |
| Accusative sg. | **in catt mbán** ‘the white cat’ (object) |
| Nominative pl. | **in chaitt báin** ‘the white cats’ |
| Accusative pl. | **inna cattu bánu** ‘the white cats’ (object) |

These forms illustrate *bán* ‘white’ as an *o*-stem adjective with masculine nouns. The adjective follows the noun and changes with its case and number.
## One point

### Read the forms: function → change

**in catt bán** ‘the white cat’ becomes genitive **in chaitt báin** ‘of the white cat’. Both noun and adjective inflect for the same case.

Old Irish adjectives usually **follow** the noun they modify. An *o/a*-class adjective uses **o-stem inflection** with masculine and neuter nouns and **a-stem inflection** with feminine nouns (we shall get to the feminine pattern later).

A simple diagnostic: compare **bán** in nominative singular with **báin** in genitive singular; the adjective changes along with *catt → chaitt*. Notice the accusative singular nasalization in **mbán**. The sentence role matters even when the bare noun looks unchanged.

In the neuter nominative/accusative plural, this adjective class uses the **long** *-a* form, not the short plural available to some neuter nouns.

*Source:* Stifter (2006), §7.4.

## Comparison (optional)

### 1. The PIE basis of gender agreement

A common Indo-European adjectival pattern uses **thematic ***-o-*** for masculine/neuter forms and ***-eh₂*** for feminine forms**. Latin *bonus / bona / bonum* illustrates a comparable inherited architecture. Old Irish *bán* belongs to an **o/a-stem adjective class** whose gender agreement has the same deep historical background (but *bán* and *bonus* need not be lexical cognates).

### 2. Why Old Irish agreement looks so different

PIE adjectives carried visible case/number/gender endings. Old Irish lost much of their **final syllabic material**, so agreement can be expressed through **final consonant quality, root-vowel changes, and mutation of a following adjective**.

| Morphological relation | Illustrative pattern | Historical level |
|---|---|---|
| adjective changes with noun case | *bán* → *báin* | inherited *agreement*, Irish phonological realization |
| masc./neut. versus fem. adjective class | *o*- versus *a*-inflection | Indo-European stem-class legacy |
| nasalization of an adjective in an accusative phrase | *mbán* | Irish morphologization of older sandhi |

### 3. Which part is innovative?

**Agreement itself is inherited**; Old Irish's **postposed attributive adjective** is a syntactic tendency (not safely reconstructible as a rigid PIE word order), and the use of **mutation alongside inflection** reflects Insular Celtic development. The system has **not become uninflected** merely because overt endings disappeared: some inflectional information has moved to neighboring sounds and words.

**References:** Stifter (2006), §7.4; [University of Texas on Old Irish adjective syntax](https://lrc.la.utexas.edu/eieol/iriol/30); Thurneysen on adjective declensions.
