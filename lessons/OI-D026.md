## Paradigm
### A compact attested verse (Deer's Cry / *Amra*-style poetic formula)

**Am gaeth i mmuir,**  
**am tond trethan.**

*‘I am wind upon the sea; I am a wave of the ocean.’*

**Reading key**

| Element | What to notice |
|---|---|
| **am** | ‘I am’ (1st-person copular form in the poetic formula) |
| **gaeth** | ‘wind’ (predicate noun) |
| **i mmuir** | ‘in the sea’ (prepositional phrase) |
| **tond** | ‘wave’ (predicate noun) |

**Source caution:** This traditional poetic formula is commonly transmitted in later manuscript forms. Spellings and editorial division vary. Treat the text above as a **normalized classroom excerpt**, not a diplomatic manuscript transcription.
## One point
Two lines, one syntactic pattern: **am + noun phrase**. The verb **am** ‘I am’ has a very different form from the W1 and W2 lexical verbs previously studied. A copular statement does not simply attach the personal endings of *caraid*.

The two lines are **parallel clauses**. Once *am* is recognized as ‘I am’, the new content is chiefly nominal: *gaeth* ‘wind’, *tond* ‘wave’, and the locative prepositional phrase *i mmuir* ‘in the sea’.

This is a **short reading preview**, not a demand to memorize the whole irregular copula paradigm. Try reading each clause, then hide the translation and reconstruct the image.

*Reference:* Stifter (2006), §13.1 includes early Irish poetic material; the excerpt above is the widely circulated *Song of Amergin* formula, in a normalized teaching form rather than asserted verbatim from Stifter.

## Comparison (optional)

### 1. A tiny poem opens a large PIE comparison

In **am gaeth** ‘I am wind’, **am** is a **copular** form. The Old Irish copula belongs to the Indo-European ***h₁es-*** ‘be’ family: compare Sanskrit **ásmi** ‘I am’ and Latin **sum** ‘I am’. The different shapes are products of **distinct histories of sound change, clitic reduction and analogical inflection**, not competing loanwords.

### 2. Irish reorganized two “be” constructions

| Old Irish verb | Core function | Comparative background |
|---|---|---|
| **am / is** (copula) | link predicate and subject | PIE ***h₁es-*** root |
| **(a)tá** (substantive verb) | existence, location, state | historically connected with ‘stand’, usually compared with PIE ***steh₂-*** |
| **ní** + conjunct copula | negation of a copular clause | additional Irish verbal syntax |

Latin **esse** versus **stāre** and Spanish **ser/estar** offer useful **typological and etymological comparisons**, but their modern grammatical distributions are **not inherited intact** from PIE into Old Irish.

### 3. What changed morphologically?

A PIE root verb underwent strong **cliticization** in Irish; the copula has short, often phonologically dependent forms and its **own absolute/conjunct behavior**. At the same time, the substantive verb became a separate paradigm for **existence and location**. The two-verb division reflects both **inherited root material** and **later functional specialization**.

The literary formula has its **own textual transmission history**; the two lines in the main lesson are a **normalized teaching excerpt**, not proof that a particular case ending or clause order existed unchanged in PIE.
