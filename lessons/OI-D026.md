## Paradigm

### Four ways to say ‘loves’ with *caraid* (W1)

| Sentence type | Form | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| statement | **caraid** | ‘(s)he loves’ |
| negative | **ní·cara** | ‘(s)he does not love’ |
| question | **in·cara** | ‘does (s)he love?’ |
| negative question | **innad·cara** | ‘does (s)he not love?’ |

*in* and *innad* are conj particles; the examples with *in* and *innad* are built by the rule of Day 14 (the verb takes conj endings after any particle). Stifter’s own example of *innad* is the question ‘Dost thou not love thy wife, man?’.

| Point | What it shows | Example |

## One point

### Verse is built differently from prose

Stifter’s poem is the third stanza of an 11th-century poem about a scribe; it is put into the mouth of Colum Cille, who lived in the 6th century and was famous as a tireless scribe. The stanza is a short quatrain; I do not reproduce it here (see Stifter §13.1). The point for the course: in verse, words are chosen partly for rhyme and syllable count, so fillers (*chevilles*) occur and the wording is special; Stifter points this out for the sentences taken from poems (sentences 16–20 of his exercise).

**Quick answers**

- **What are the new prepositions in Lesson 13?** *cen*, *co*, *di*, *fo*, *fri*, *ó*, *tar*; they are taught on Day 30.
- **Where do questions come from in the text?** From the particle *in* (statement → question) and *innad* (negative question); both take conj endings (§9.1, §13.2).
- **Why does *fo* mean two things?** *fo* with the prep means ‘(placed) under’; with the acc, ‘(going) under’ (Day 30).
