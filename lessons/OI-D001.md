## Paradigm

Mutation of the next word: [L] lenition · [N] nasalization · [H] aspiration (h- before a vowel, gemination of a consonant) · [0] none. The letters show how the *next* word is mutated, not how the noun itself changes.

### *ech* ‘horse’ · masc o-stem · sg

| Case | Sg form + effect |
|---|---|
| Nom | **ech** [0] |
| Gen | **eich** [L] |
| Prep | **euch / eoch** [L] |
| Acc | **ech** [N] |
| Voc | **a eich** [L] |

The **prepositional case (prep)** is Stifter’s name for the case used after prepositions (other grammars call it the dative, dat). In Old Irish it is the one case that replaces the PIE dat, instrumental and ablative. *Eoch* is only a spelling of *euch* (after *e*, u-infection may be written *o*; p. 43, note 1). The voc particle *a* lenites a following consonant; *ech* begins with a vowel, so nothing shows.

## One point

### A lost ending leaves a mutation on the next word

**Nom and acc are both *ech*.** After the final syllables were lost, the case was marked on the following word. The kind of mutation depends on the sound that was lost:

| Lost final sound | Mutation of the next word |
|---|---|
| a vowel (\*-ī, \*-ū, \*-e) | lenition [L] |
| a nasal (\*-n < \*-m) | nasalization [N] |
| \*-s after a long vowel | aspiration [H] (Day 2) |

Stifter's own phrases with *becc* ‘small’ show it: nom *int ech becc*, gen *ind eich bicc*, acc *in n-ech mbecc* (*in*, *ind*, *int* are forms of the article, Day 8).

**Quick answers**

- **Why is the nom [0], although its old ending \*-ah ended in *h*?** Neither Stifter nor EIEOL explains it. In Stifter's tables [H] follows only *long vowel + h* (\*-ūh, \*-āh); the short \*-ah leaves nothing.
- **Why can ech be both nom and acc?** The case is no longer in the noun; it is in the mutation of the next word and in the role of the noun in the sentence.
- **What is u-infection?** Stifter’s term for the vowel *u* that a lost final *-u* leaves in the last syllable (\*exʷū > *euch*).
- **Why is the case called “prep” in some tables?** Short for prepositional, Stifter's name for the case that other grammars call the dative (dat); it is the case used after prepositions such as *do*, *ar*, *i* (Day 19).

## Comparison (optional)

### Historical development: how did \*h₁éḱu̯os become *ech*?

| Case | PIE | Proto-Celtic | Primitive Irish | Old Irish |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nom | \*h₁éḱu̯os | \*ekʷos | \*exʷah | **ech** [0] |
| Gen | \*h₁éḱu̯os(i̯o) | \*ekʷī « | \*exʷī | **eich** [L] |
| Prep | \*h₁éḱu̯ōi̯, \*-oh₁, \*-ōd | \*ekʷūi̯, \*-ū, \*-ūð | \*exʷū | **euch** [L] |
| Acc | \*h₁éḱu̯om | \*ekʷom | \*exʷan | **ech** [N] |
| Voc | \*h₁éḱu̯e | \*ekʷe | \*exʷe | **a eich** [L] |

« = not a regular sound change; the form was replaced by analogy (Stifter's mark). Forms from Stifter p. 43; his symbol for /n/ is written *n* here.

1. **The root.** Celtic merged PIE \*ḱ with \*k, so \*ḱu̯ > \*kʷ (Sanskrit *áśva-* keeps the palatal as *ś*). Between vowels \*kʷ became \*xʷ in Primitive Irish; Old Irish writes *ch*. At the start of a word \*kʷ gave *c* (*cenn*, Day 4).
2. **The endings.** The final syllables were lost between Primitive and Old Irish. What survived: the palatalization of the consonant before \*-ī and \*-e (*eich*), u-infection before \*-ū (*euch*), and the mutation of the next word (vowel → [L], \*-n → [N]).
3. **The gen («).** PIE \*-osi̯o was replaced by \*-ī, the ending Latin also has (*equī*). Whether this is a shared Italo-Celtic innovation is discussed on Day 7.
4. **One prep from three cases.** PIE dat \*-ōi, instrumental \*-oh₁ and ablative \*-ōd all became \*-ū.

**Summary.** Regular sound change: 1, 2, 4. Analogy: the gen (3). Open: why the nom leaves no [H].
