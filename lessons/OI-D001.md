## Paradigm

| Case | Form | Following-word effect |
|---|---|---|
| Nominative sg. | **ech** | L |
| Genitive sg. | **eich** | L |
| Prepositional sg. | **euch** / *eoch* | L |
| Accusative sg. | **ech** | N |
| Vocative sg. | **a eich** | L |

L and N indicate effects on a following word in the textbook paradigm, not changes to the spelling of *ech*.

## One point

The bare form *ech* is shared by the nominative and accusative singular. In its phrase, however, the mutation pattern affecting the next word can distinguish them. The genitive *eich* and prepositional *euch / eoch* show other case shapes.

## Comparison (optional)

| Language | Nominative sg. | Accusative sg. |
|---|---|---|
| Latin | *equus* | *equum* |
| Sanskrit | *aśvaḥ* | *aśvam* |
| Old Irish | *ech* | *ech* |

These are cognate words (PIE \*h₁éḱʷos ‘horse’). Latin and Sanskrit retain a visible nom.–acc. difference in the noun; the corresponding bare Old Irish forms coincide. This is a comparison of inflectional patterns, not a claim that the Irish form derives from the Latin one.

*Evidence:* textbook paradigm (Stifter 2006, §6.4); not every cell is asserted to be individually attested in a surviving manuscript.
