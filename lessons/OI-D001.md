## Paradigm

Following-word effects: [L] lenition · [N] nasalization · [H] aspiration (h- before a vowel, gemination of a consonant) · [0] none. They show how the *next* word is mutated, not how the noun itself changes.

### *ech* ‘horse’ · masculine o-stem · singular

| Case | Singular form + effect |
|---|---|
| Nominative | **ech** [0] |
| Genitive | **eich** [L] |
| Dative | **euch / eoch** [L] |
| Accusative | **ech** [N] |
| Vocative | **a eich** [L] |

Stifter calls the dative “prepositional”; it is the one case that replaces PIE dative, instrumental and ablative. *Eoch* is only a spelling of *euch* (after *e*, u-infection may be written *o*; p. 43, note 1). The vocative particle *a* lenites a following consonant; *ech* begins with a vowel, so nothing shows.

## One point

### A lost ending leaves a mutation on the next word

**Nominative and accusative are both *ech*.** After the final syllables were lost, the case was marked on the following word. The kind of mutation depends on the sound that was lost:

| Lost final sound | Mutation of the next word |
|---|---|
| a vowel (\*-ī, \*-ū, \*-e) | lenition [L] |
| a nasal (\*-n < \*-m) | nasalization [N] |
| \*-s after a long vowel | aspiration [H] (Day 2) |

Stifter's own phrases with *becc* ‘small’ show it: nominative *int ech becc*, genitive *ind eich bicc*, accusative *in n-ech mbecc* (*in*, *ind*, *int* are forms of the article, Day 8).

**Quick answers**

- **Why is the nominative [0], although its old ending \*-ah ended in *h*?** Neither Stifter nor EIEOL explains it. In Stifter's tables [H] follows only *long vowel + h* (\*-ūh, \*-āh); the short \*-ah leaves nothing.
- **Why can ech be both nominative and accusative?** The case is no longer in the noun; it is in the mutation of the next word and in the role of the noun in the sentence.
- **Why is the case called “prepositional” in some tables?** Stifter's name for the dative; the dative is the case used after prepositions such as *do*, *ar*, *i* (Day 19).

## Comparison (optional)

### Historical development: how did \*h₁éḱu̯os become *ech*?

| Case | PIE | Proto-Celtic | Primitive Irish | Old Irish |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nominative | \*h₁éḱu̯os | \*ekʷos | \*exʷah | **ech** [0] |
| Genitive | \*h₁éḱu̯os(i̯o) | \*ekʷī « | \*exʷī | **eich** [L] |
| Dative | \*h₁éḱu̯ōi̯, \*-oh₁, \*-ōd | \*ekʷūi̯, \*-ū, \*-ūð | \*exʷū | **euch** [L] |
| Accusative | \*h₁éḱu̯om | \*ekʷom | \*exʷan | **ech** [N] |
| Vocative | \*h₁éḱu̯e | \*ekʷe | \*exʷe | **a eich** [L] |

« = not a regular sound change; the form was replaced by analogy (Stifter's mark). Forms from Stifter p. 43; his symbol for /n/ is written *n* here.

1. **The root.** Celtic merged PIE \*ḱ with \*k, so \*ḱu̯ > \*kʷ (Sanskrit *áśva-* keeps the palatal as *ś*). Between vowels \*kʷ became \*xʷ in Primitive Irish; Old Irish writes *ch*. At the start of a word \*kʷ gave *c* (*cenn*, Day 4).
2. **The endings.** The final syllables were lost between Primitive and Old Irish. What survived: the palatalization of the consonant before \*-ī and \*-e (*eich*), u-infection before \*-ū (*euch*), and the mutation of the next word (vowel → [L], \*-n → [N]).
3. **The genitive («).** PIE \*-osi̯o was replaced by \*-ī, the ending Latin also has (*equī*). Whether this is a shared Italo-Celtic innovation is discussed on Day 7.
4. **One dative from three cases.** PIE dative \*-ōi, instrumental \*-oh₁ and ablative \*-ōd all became \*-ū.

**Summary.** Regular sound change: 1, 2, 4. Analogy: the genitive (3). Unresolved: why the nominative leaves no [H].
