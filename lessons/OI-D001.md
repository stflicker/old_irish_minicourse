## Paradigm

Following-word effects: [L] lenition · [N] nasalization · [H] aspiration / gemination · [0] none. They describe the *next* word, not the noun's own ending.

### *ech* ‘horse’ · masculine o-stem · singular

| Case | Singular form + effect |
|---|---|
| Nominative | **ech** [0] |
| Genitive | **eich** [L] |
| Prepositional | **euch / eoch** [L] |
| Accusative | **ech** [N] |
| Vocative | **a eich** [L] |

*Eoch* is only a spelling of *euch* (after *e*, u-infection may be written *o*; Stifter p. 43, note 1). The vocative particle *a* lenites a following consonant; *ech* begins with a vowel, so nothing shows.

## One point

### The mutation is the fossil of the lost ending

**Nominative and accusative are both *ech*.** The case signal moved to the next word. After the final syllables were lost, an old ending still left a mark on the following word:

| The lost ending was… | The next word is… |
|---|---|
| a vowel (\*-ī, \*-ū, \*-e) | lenited [L] |
| a nasal (\*-n < \*-m) | nasalized [N] |
| \*-s after a long vowel | aspirated [H] (Day 2) |

You can see it in Stifter's own phrases with *becc* ‘small’: nom. *int ech becc*, gen. *ind eich bicc*, acc. *in n-ech mbecc* (*in/ind/int* is the article, Day 8).

**Quick answers**

- **Why is the nominative [0], although its old ending \*-ah ended in h?** Neither Stifter nor EIEOL explains it. In Stifter's tables [H] follows only *long vowel + h* (\*-ūh, \*-āh); the short \*-ah leaves nothing.
- **Why “prepositional”?** It is Stifter's name for the dative: the case after prepositions such as *do*, *ar*, *i* (Day 19).
- **Why do I not see a dual?** This course skips duals, as Stifter does in his exercises (§6.7).

## Comparison (optional)

### Historical card: how did \*h₁éḱu̯os become *ech*?

| Cell | PIE | Proto-Celtic | Primitive Irish | Old Irish |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nominative | \*h₁éḱu̯os | \*ekʷos | \*exʷah | **ech** [0] |
| Genitive | \*h₁éḱu̯os(i̯o) | \*ekʷī « | \*exʷī | **eich** [L] |
| Prepositional | \*h₁éḱu̯ōi̯, \*-oh₁, \*-ōd | \*ekʷūi̯, \*-ū, \*-ūð | \*exʷū | **euch** [L] |
| Accusative | \*h₁éḱu̯om | \*ekʷom | \*exʷan | **ech** [N] |
| Vocative | \*h₁éḱu̯e | \*ekʷe | \*exʷe | **a eich** [L] |

« = not a regular sound change: the form was replaced by analogy (Stifter's mark). Forms from Stifter p. 43; his symbol for /n/ is written *n* here.

1. **The root.** Celtic merged PIE \*ḱ with \*k, so \*ḱu̯ > \*kʷ (Sanskrit *áśva-* keeps the palatal as ś). Between vowels \*kʷ was lenited to \*xʷ in Primitive Irish; Old Irish writes *ch*. At the start of a word \*kʷ gave *c* instead (*cenn*, Day 4).
2. **The endings.** The final syllables were lost between Primitive and Old Irish. What survived: the consonant before the ending is palatalized by \*-ī and \*-e (*eich*) or rounded by \*-ū (*euch* = u-infection), and the next word is mutated (vowel → [L], \*-n → [N]).
3. **The genitive was replaced («).** PIE \*-osi̯o was replaced by \*-ī, the ending Latin also has (*equī*). The sources agree that this is not a shared Italo-Celtic innovation (Weiss, cited below).
4. **One case from three.** PIE dative \*-ōi, instrumental \*-oh₁ and ablative \*-ōd all became \*-ū, hence one “prepositional” case.

**Status.** Regular: 1, 2, 4. Analogical: the genitive (3). Open: why the nominative leaves no [H].

*Sources:* Stifter (2006) §6.4, p. 43 and §4.1, §4.7 (mutations); [EIEOL, Old Irish Online, Grammar §6](https://lrc.la.utexas.edu/eieol/iriol/20#grammar_1275) (lenition after a final vowel, nasalization after final \*-n, aspiration after final \*-s); PIE \*h₁éḱu̯os > Proto-Celtic \*ekʷos: Matasović (2009) as quoted in Wiktionary (not checked directly); Weiss on the \*-ī genitive in *The Indo-European Language Family* (Cambridge), ch. “Italo-Celtic” (summary only).
