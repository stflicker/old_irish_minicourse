## Paradigm

| Case | Form | Following-word effect |
|---|---|---|
| Nominative sg. | **ech** | — |
| Genitive sg. | **eich** | L |
| Prepositional sg. | **euch** / *eoch* | L |
| Accusative sg. | **ech** | N |
| Vocative sg. | **a eich** | L |

Here **—** marks no case-triggered mutation of a following word; **L** means lenition and **N** means nasalization. These are phrase-level effects, not changes to the spelling of *ech*.

## One point

The bare form *ech* is shared by the nominative and accusative singular. The masculine nominative singular does not itself trigger mutation of a following adjective, whereas the accusative singular has an N effect. This phrase-level contrast can distinguish otherwise identical noun forms. The genitive *eich* and prepositional *euch / eoch* show other case shapes.

## Comparison (optional)

### 1. The inherited thematic opposition

PIE masculine thematic nouns had a stem vowel ***-o-*** followed by case endings. For the cognate ‘horse’ (PIE ***h₁éḱwos***), the contrast is easiest to see in three languages:

| Historical category | PIE thematic ending | Latin | Sanskrit | Old Irish |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nominative singular | ***-os*** | *equus* | *aśvaḥ* | *ech* |
| Accusative singular | ***-om*** | *equum* | *aśvam* | *ech* |

The table compares **case morphology and cognate lexemes**, not a direct descent of Irish from Latin or Sanskrit. The PIE forms are reconstructions (marked by `*`), not written attestations.

### 2. What changed in Celtic and Irish?

At an early stage, **nominative *-s*** and **accusative *-m*** had different phonological consequences. Extensive reductions of final syllables ultimately gave the **same isolated Old Irish noun** *ech* in both cases. Yet the lost accusative nasal helps explain why that case may have a **nasalizing effect on a following word**. Thus the *phonological outcome* merged while parts of the *morphosyntactic distinction* survived elsewhere in the phrase.

### 3. Inheritance versus innovation

The **nominative/accusative contrast is inherited**; the use of a **following-word mutation to signal a now invisible distinction** is an Irish development rooted in earlier sandhi. Compare Sanskrit and Latin, which still display different word-final forms. This is a model of *exponence migration*: a category once carried by an ending can become visible on an adjacent constituent. Do not read the N marker as a second, independent PIE suffix.
