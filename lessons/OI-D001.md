## Paradigm

| Case | Form | Following-word effect |
|---|---|---|
| Nominative sg. | **ech** | — |
| Genitive sg. | **eich** | L |
| Prepositional sg. | **euch** / *eoch* | L |
| Accusative sg. | **ech** | N |
| Vocative sg. | **a eich** | L |

Here **—** marks no case-triggered mutation of a following word; **L** means lenition and **N** means nasalization. These are phrase-level effects, not changes to the spelling of *ech*.

## One point

The bare form *ech* is shared by the nominative and accusative singular. The masculine nominative singular does not itself trigger mutation of a following adjective, whereas the accusative singular has an N effect. This phrase-level contrast can distinguish otherwise identical noun forms. The genitive *eich* and prepositional *euch / eoch* show other case shapes.

## Comparison (optional)

| Language | Nominative sg. | Accusative sg. |
|---|---|---|
| Latin | *equus* | *equum* |
| Sanskrit | *aśvaḥ* | *aśvam* |
| Old Irish | *ech* | *ech* |

These are cognate words (PIE \*h₁éḱʷos ‘horse’). Latin and Sanskrit retain a visible nom.–acc. difference in the noun; the corresponding bare Old Irish forms coincide. This is a comparison of inflectional patterns, not a claim that the Irish form derives from the Latin one.

*Evidence:* Stifter (2006), §6.4; compare Thurneysen, *A Grammar of Old Irish*, §232 on the environments for postnominal lenition (masculine genitive, vocative, and nominative plural, not nominative singular). Not every cell is asserted to be individually attested in a surviving manuscript.
