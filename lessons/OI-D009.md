## Paradigm

Mutation of the next word: [L] lenition · [N] nasalization · [H] aspiration (h- before a vowel, gemination of a consonant) · [0] none. The letters show how the *next* word is mutated, not how the article itself changes.

### The article ‘the’ · masc · pl (before b, d, g, p, t, c, m)

| Case | Pl article + effect | Example phrase |
|---|---|---|
| Nom | **in** [L] | **in chaitt** ‘the cats’ |
| Gen | **inna** [N] | **inna catt** ‘of the cats’ |
| Prep | **-(s)naib** [0] | **donaib cattaib** ‘to the cats’ |
| Acc | **inna** [H] | **inna chattu** ‘the cats’ (object) |
| Voc | **a** [L] (particle; no article) | **a chattu** ‘O cats’ |

### The variants of the article, case by case

| Case | Next word begins with b, d, g, p, t, c, m | a vowel | l, r, n, f | s |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nom sg | **in** [0] | **int** [0] | **in** [0] | **in** [0] |
| Gen sg | **in** [L] | **ind** [L] | **ind** [L] | **int** [L] |
| Prep sg | **-(s)in** [L] | **-(s)ind** [L] | **-(s)ind** [L] | **-(s)int** [L] |
| Acc sg | **in** [N] | **in** [N] | **in** [N] | **in** [N] |
| Nom pl | **in** [L] | **ind** [L] | **ind** [L] | **int** [L] |
| Gen pl | **inna** [N] | **inna** [N] | **inna** [N] | **inna** [N] |
| Prep pl | **-(s)naib** [0] | **-(s)naib** [0] | **-(s)naib** [0] | **-(s)naib** [0] |
| Acc pl | **inna** [H] | **inna** [H] | **inna** [H] | **inna** [H] |

Only four rows change with the first sound of the next word: nom sg, gen sg, prep sg and nom pl. The others are the same in every column.

### The variants with examples

| Case | vowel: *en* ‘bird’ | l: *lóech* ‘warrior’ | r: *rían* ‘sea’ | n: *níul* ‘cloud’ | f: *fer* ‘man’ | s: *sacart* ‘priest’ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nom sg | **int en** | **in lóech** † | **in rían** † | **in níul** † | **in fer** † | **in sacart** † |
| Gen sg and nom pl | **ind euin** | **ind lóich** | **ind rein** | **ind níuil** | **ind fir** | **int sacairt** |
| Prep sg | **dond eun** | **cosind lóech** | **arind rían** | **isind níul** | **fond fiur** | **dont sacart**, **cosint sacart** |

† Built by the rule: before everything except a vowel, the nom sg is *in*. The other forms are Stifter’s examples (§7.2); the f of *fer* is lost after *ind* (lenition), so *ind fir* is pronounced like *ind ir*.

## One point

### The pl article is again the o-stem pattern

The mutations of the pl article are those of the o-stem pl: the nom pl lenites [L], the gen pl nasalizes [N], the prep pl has no effect [0], the acc pl aspirates [H]. Which spelling the article takes (*in*, *int*, *ind*) depends only on the first sound of the next word, and only in four places: before a vowel the nom sg is *int*; before a vowel, *l*, *r*, *n* or *f* the gen sg, nom pl and prep sg end in *-d*; before *s* they end in *-t*, and the lenited *s* is pronounced *h* (*int sacairt* is pronounced like *int hagart*).

**Quick answers**

- **How do I tell the gen sg from the nom pl?** In the article and the noun they are often identical (*ind euin* ‘of the bird’ and ‘the birds’; *in chaitt* ‘of the cat’ and ‘the cats’). The role of the phrase in the sentence decides.
- **When is the article spelled *int*?** In the nom sg before a vowel (*int en*), and in the gen sg and nom pl before *s* (*int sacairt*).
- **What does the *-d* of *ind* do to the next word?** The article lenites the next word. *F* disappears (*ind fir*), *s* becomes *h* (written *int sacairt*), and a vowel stays a vowel.
- **What do the prep pl forms look like after prepositions?** The preposition and *-(s)naib* fuse: *donaib* ‘to the’, *isnaib* ‘in the’, *arnaib* ‘in front of the’ (Stifter §7.1 note 2).
- **Why is *inna* sometimes written *na*?** In the oldest texts *inna* has two syllables; the most common form is *na* (§7.1 note 5).
- **Why *ind* before a vowel and before *l, r, n, f*?** Stifter lists these sounds without a reason. Toner’s study of the article in place-names (*Nomina* 22) confirms the pattern for the gen: the older *-d* was kept before vowels and before lenited *f, r, l, n*; he calls the *-d* “original” but does not say where it comes from. The pattern was given up by the twelfth century. The standard descriptions give no reason for exactly these sounds, so treat the list as a rule to learn.
