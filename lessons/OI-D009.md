## Paradigm
| Case | Article | Example with **fer** |
|---|---|---|
| Nominative plural | **in** (L; **ind** before *f*) | **ind fir** |
| Genitive plural | **inna** (N) | **inna fer** |
| Accusative plural | **inna** (H) | **inna firu** |
| Prepositional plural | **-(s)naib** | **donaib feraib** |

In early Old Irish, full **inna** is found; shortened **na** is frequent later. Article variation depends partly on the initial sound of the noun.
## One point
The most useful plural opposition is **nominative in** versus **accusative inna**. In practice, before *fer* the nominative often looks like **ind fir**; genitive plural is **inna fer**, accusative plural **inna firu**.

Two identical articles, **inna**, do **not** erase the distinction between genitive and accusative plural. The **noun form** and following-word effects must also be considered.

One more variant worth recognizing: masculine nominative singular **int** can occur before a vowel, as in **int en** ‘the bird’. Do not generalize *int* to all noun initials.

*Source:* Stifter (2006), §§7.1–7.2.

## Comparison (optional)

### 1. PIE demonstratives supplied an inflectional template

Early Indo-European deictics had specialized plural forms and case distinctions, which helped Indo-European descendants create articles. Old Irish plural **in/ind** and **inna** are descendants of a **restructured Celtic demonstrative paradigm**, not plural suffixes tacked onto a noun.

The conversion of a demonstrative to an article is a **grammaticalization pathway**: its earlier pointing/anaphoric meaning weakens, while its distribution as a determiner becomes more regular.

### 2. Forms can merge while agreement survives

| Phrase | Syntactic role | Historical diagnostic |
|---|---|---|
| **ind fir** | masc. nominative plural | article variant + palatalized nominal plural |
| **inna fer** | masc. genitive plural | genitive context and nasalizing morphology |
| **inna firu** | masc. accusative plural | accusative **-u** on the noun |

The article **inna** is **not an accusative suffix**: it occurs in multiple cells. Its apparent syncretism was shaped by Celtic phonological reduction and remodeling of former pronominal distinctions.

### 3. Innovation at the interface of syntax and phonology

Older case endings on determiners and nouns sometimes left **mutation effects**; other cells became homophonous. As a result, Old Irish is best analyzed at the level of **whole noun phrases**. One important innovation is that *case exponence* can be spread across separate words, rather than residing in one overt ending.

**References:** Stifter (2006), §§7.1–7.2; [Luján, “From demonstratives to articles in the Celtic languages”](https://benjamins.com/catalog/jhl.24033.luj); Thurneysen on pronominal and article flexion.
