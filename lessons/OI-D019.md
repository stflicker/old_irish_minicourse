## Paradigm
| Preposition | With article, singular prepositional | Plural prepositional |
|---|---|---|
| **do** ‘to, for’ | **don** / **dond** | **donaib** |
| **i** ‘in’ | **isin** / **isind** | **isnaib** |
| **fo** ‘under’ | **fon** / **fond** | **fonaib** |

The precise final consonant can vary with the next noun's initial sound. This table illustrates **preposition + article**, not the preposition with a possessive.
## One point
With an ordinary prepositional noun phrase, the definite article typically **fuses with the preposition**. Compare **do** ‘to, for’ with **don** ‘to/for the’; and **i** ‘in’ with **isin** ‘in the’.

One short diagnostic is whether a preposition favors the **s-containing** article variant. With **do**, we normally see *don*, but with **i**, *isin*. There are conditioned variants and exceptions, so don't turn this into an unrestricted *s*-rule.

In a phrase such as **dond fiur** ‘for the man’, you can segment the structure as *do + definite article + prepositional singular noun* even though it is written in only two words.

*Source:* Stifter (2006), §11.3; examples also in §7.1.

## Comparison (optional)

### 1. From an inflected demonstrative to a fused article

PIE had **case government by adpositions**, demonstrative forms and clitics, but **not** the particular Old Irish **preposition + definite article** compounds. Once the Celtic demonstrative became a routine article, frequent sequences were **reanalyzed as single words**.

### 2. A schematic chain

| Earlier syntactic combination | Old Irish compound | Grammatical result |
|---|---|---|
| **do** ‘to/for’ + article | **don / dond** | one unit introducing a defined oblique noun |
| **i** ‘in’ + article | **isin / isind** | preposition and determiner fused |
| **fo** ‘under’ + article | **fon / fond** | another contracted form |

This is **morphological fusion**: two once separate grammatical elements become a single phonological/morphological word. The alternation of *-n/-nd* depends partly on the following noun, rather than marking a different PIE case.

### 3. What's specifically Celtic or Irish?

The **inflectional case system** and the **prepositions** have older Indo-European ancestry. But the **obligatory combinations with a grammaticalized definite article**, their conditioned variants and the noun mutations that accompany them arose **after Celtic diversified from PIE**.

An analogy in familiar languages is Latin *in* + Romance article *il* → Italian *nel* (historically *in + il*), illustrating the **type of process** rather than a common inherited form.

**References:** Stifter (2006), §11.3; Thurneysen on article–preposition compounds; [Luján, “From demonstratives to articles in the Celtic languages”](https://benjamins.com/catalog/jhl.24033.luj).
