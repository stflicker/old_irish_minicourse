## Paradigm

Following-word effects: [L] lenition · [N] nasalization · [H] aspiration (h- before a vowel, gemination of a consonant) · [0] none. They show how the *next* word is mutated, not how the noun itself changes.

### *ech* ‘horse’ · masculine o-stem

| Case | Singular form + effect | Plural form + effect |
|---|---|---|
| Nominative | **ech** [0] | **eich** [L] |
| Genitive | **eich** [L] | **ech** [N] |
| Dative | **euch / eoch** [L] | **echaib** [0] |
| Accusative | **ech** [N] | **echu / euchu / eochu** [H] |
| Vocative | **a eich** [L] | **a echu / a euchu / a eochu** [H] |

## One point

### Read the plural from the ending and the mutation together

Two plural forms are distinct by spelling (*echaib*, *echu*); two are not (*eich*, *ech*). For these the mutation of the next word decides:

- **eich** [L]: nominative plural or genitive singular (the sentence decides which).
- **ech** [N]: genitive plural or accusative singular (again, the sentence decides).
- **echu** [H]: accusative plural, and the vocative plural copied from it.

**Quick answers**

- **Where does [H] come from?** From the old accusative plural ending \*-ūs: the vowel and the \*-s were lost, the aspiration stayed. Written evidence: after the article *inna* (acc. pl.) Stifter (§4.7) has *inna mmaccu* ‘the sons’, with the initial doubled. EIEOL (§6.3) lists the accusative plural of all genders among the aspirating forms but notes that gemination is written only where two words are joined, so examples after a noun and before an adjective are rare.
- **Why is the vocative plural *a echu*, not *a eich*?** Old Irish copies the accusative plural into the vocative («, Stifter p. 43).
- **Why *echaib* with no [H], although \*-ih also ended in *h*?** In Stifter's tables [H] follows only *long vowel + h* (\*-ūh, \*-āh); short \*-ih leaves nothing. This is a pattern in the tables, not a rule Stifter states.
- **Variants *euchu* / *eochu*:** u-infection again (p. 43, note 1).

## Comparison (optional)

### Historical development: why is the plural a mixture of old and new?

| Case | PIE | Proto-Celtic | Primitive Irish | Old Irish |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nominative | \*h₁éḱu̯ōs | \*ekʷoi̯ « | \*exʷī | **eich** [L] |
| Genitive | \*h₁éḱu̯ōm | \*ekʷom | \*exʷan | **ech** [N] |
| Dative | \*h₁éḱu̯ōis, \*-obʰos | \*ekʷobis « | \*exʷaβih | **echaib** [0] |
| Accusative | \*h₁éḱu̯ons | \*ekʷūs | \*exʷūh | **echu** [H] |
| Vocative | \*h₁éḱu̯ōs | \*ekʷoi̯ « | \*exʷī | **a echu** [H] « |

Forms from Stifter p. 43. « = replaced by analogy, not a regular sound change.

1. **Nominative plural («).** PIE \*-ōs was replaced by \*-oi, the ending of the pronouns (Latin *equī*, Greek *híppoi*; Sanskrit *áśvāḥ* keeps \*-ōs). In Primitive Irish \*-oi̯ and the genitive singular \*-ī merged as \*-ī: hence *eich* twice.
2. **Accusative plural (regular).** \*-ons > \*-ūs: the nasal fell before \*-s and the vowel was lengthened (Latin *equōs* likewise; Sanskrit *áśvān* keeps the *n*). Then \*-s > \*-h, and the final syllable was lost, leaving [H].
3. **Genitive plural (regular).** \*-ōm shortened to \*-om, so it is identical with the accusative singular: both [N].
4. **Dative and vocative plural («).** \*-obis (from the PIE instrumental plural \*-obʰos) replaced \*-ōis in the dative. The vocative plural takes over the accusative form instead of the regular \*-ī.

**Summary.** Regular sound change: 2, 3. Analogy («): nominative, dative and vocative plural. Unresolved: why only a long vowel + \*-s leaves [H].
