## Paradigm

**Following-word effects:** [L] lenition; [N] nasalization; [H] historical aspiration/gemination; [0] no regular L/N/H effect prescribed here. The marker belongs to the form before it and describes the next closely connected word, not a suffix. Effects need not be visible in spelling; phonological and syntactic restrictions still apply. Prepositional = the case often called dative. The vocative particle a itself lenites the noun; a marker at the end of the vocative phrase describes what follows the noun.

### *ech* ‘horse’ · masculine o-stem

| Case | Singular form + effect | Plural form + effect |
|---|---|---|
| Nominative | **ech** [0] | **eich** [L] |
| Genitive | **eich** [L] | **ech** [N] |
| Prepositional | **euch / eoch** [L] | **echaib** [0] |
| Accusative | **ech** [N] | **echu / euchu / eochu** [H] |
| Vocative | **a eich** [L] | **a echu / a euchu / a eochu** [H] |

The spelling eoch/eochu is an alternative to euch/euchu; it is not an extra case.

## One point

### Read the forms: function → change

**Form:** *eich* (nominative plural) versus *echu / euchu* (accusative plural). **Function:** subject versus object in the plural. **Change:** the ending and sometimes the root vowel distinguish the two. Do not confuse the following-word markers with written endings.

Yesterday's **nominative and accusative singular were both *ech***. The plural is different: nominative **eich** versus accusative **echu/euchu**. That opposition is a useful recognition test in a real sentence.

Compare *eich* ‘horses’ (nominative plural) with *eich* ‘of a horse’ (genitive singular). You cannot infer case and number from this form alone.

An easy memory pair: **eich** is nominative plural, **echu** is accusative plural; **echaib** is the prepositional plural.

*Source:* Stifter (2006), §6.4, standard masculine *o*-stem paradigm.

## Comparison (optional)

### 1. Where the plural endings came from

The PIE masculine thematic paradigm did not have a single undifferentiated “plural ending.” Different cases had different suffixes, and some forms underwent early analogical replacement.

| Grammatical category | Comparative starting point | Old Irish *ech* |
|---|---|---|
| Nominative plural | ***-oi*** (originally prominent in pronominal inflection, spread to nouns) | **eich** |
| Accusative plural | thematic accusative ***-ons*** | **echu / euchu** |
| Genitive plural | old genitive plural in a nasal-final ending (often ***-ōm*** in PIE reconstruction) | **ech**, with N effect |
| Prepositional plural | older oblique plural morphology with a *-b(h)-* element | **echaib** |

The **nominative *-oi*** is especially interesting: as widely discussed for Italic and Celtic, **an originally pronominal inflectional ending was generalized to nouns**. This is *analogical innovation within an inherited case system*.

### 2. Why are the endings so different?

Later reduction of unstressed syllables left **palatal quality** as a major signal of nominative plural (*eich*), whereas the accusative plural retained an audible **-u** (*echu*). The genitive plural can look identical to the nominative singular (*ech*) but retains the *nasalizing grammatical environment* associated with its older nasal.

### 3. The historical lesson

Do not equate *eich* and *echu* with two arbitrary modern endings. They are **different descendants of differently formed prehistoric plurals**, reshaped by sound change and analogical spread. Latin *equī/equōs* is valuable for the **formal category contrast**, but the complete intermediate Celtic history is not recoverable from Latin alone.
