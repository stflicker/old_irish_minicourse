## Paradigm

Following-word effects: [L] lenition · [N] nasalization · [H] aspiration / gemination · [0] none. They describe the *next* word, not the noun's own ending.

### *ech* ‘horse’ · masculine o-stem

| Case | Singular form + effect | Plural form + effect |
|---|---|---|
| Nominative | **ech** [0] | **eich** [L] |
| Genitive | **eich** [L] | **ech** [N] |
| Prepositional | **euch / eoch** [L] | **echaib** [0] |
| Accusative | **ech** [N] | **echu / euchu / eochu** [H] |
| Vocative | **a eich** [L] | **a echu / a euchu / a eochu** [H] |

## One point

### One plural spelling, two histories; one [H], one lost \*-s

**Read the plural by its mutation.** *eich* [L] is nominative plural or genitive singular (context decides); *ech* [N] is accusative singular or genitive plural; *echu* [H] is accusative (or vocative) plural.

**Quick answers**

- **Why are there two *eich*?** Different cells, different endings, one result: both ended in Primitive Irish \*-ī (Historical card, 1).
- **Where does the [H] come from?** From the old accusative plural ending \*-ūs: the vowel and the \*-s were lost, the aspiration stayed. Written evidence: after the article *inna* (acc. pl.) Stifter (§4.7) quotes *inna mmaccu* ‘the sons’, with the initial doubled. EIEOL (§6.3) lists the accusative plural of all genders among the aspirating forms but notes that gemination is written only where two words are joined, so direct examples after a noun and before an adjective are rare.
- **Why is the vocative plural *a echu*, not *a eich*?** Old Irish copies the accusative plural into the vocative («, Stifter p. 43).
- **Why *echaib* with no [H], though \*-ih also ended in h?** In Stifter's tables [H] follows only *long vowel + h* (\*-ūh, \*-āh); short \*-ih leaves nothing. This is a pattern in the tables, not a rule stated by Stifter.
- **Variants *euchu* / *eochu*:** u-infection again (p. 43, note 1).

## Comparison (optional)

### Historical card: why is the plural a mixture of old and new?

| Cell | PIE | Proto-Celtic | Primitive Irish | Old Irish |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nominative | \*h₁éḱu̯ōs | \*ekʷoi̯ « | \*exʷī | **eich** [L] |
| Genitive | \*h₁éḱu̯ōm | \*ekʷom | \*exʷan | **ech** [N] |
| Prepositional | \*h₁éḱu̯ōis, \*-obʰos | \*ekʷobis « | \*exʷaβih | **echaib** [0] |
| Accusative | \*h₁éḱu̯ons | \*ekʷūs | \*exʷūh | **echu** [H] |
| Vocative | \*h₁éḱu̯ōs | \*ekʷoi̯ « | \*exʷī | **a echu** [H] « |

Forms from Stifter p. 43. « = replaced by analogy, not a regular sound change.

1. **Nominative plural («).** PIE \*-ōs was replaced by \*-oi, the ending of the pronouns (Latin *equī*, Greek *híppoi*; Sanskrit *áśvāḥ* keeps \*-ōs). In Primitive Irish \*-oi̯ and the genitive singular \*-ī merged as \*-ī: hence *eich* twice.
2. **Accusative plural (regular).** \*-ons > \*-ūs: the nasal fell before \*-s and the vowel was lengthened (Latin *equōs* likewise; Sanskrit *áśvān* keeps the *n*). Then \*-s > \*-h, and the final syllable was lost, leaving [H].
3. **Genitive plural (regular).** \*-ōm shortened to \*-om, so it is identical with the accusative singular: both [N].
4. **Dative plural and vocative («).** \*-obis (from the PIE instrumental plural \*-obʰos) replaced \*-ōis. The vocative plural takes over the accusative form.

**Status.** Regular: 2, 3. Replaced («): nominative, dative and vocative plural. Open: why only long-vowel + \*-s leaves [H].

*Sources:* Stifter (2006) §6.4 p. 43, §4.7; [EIEOL Grammar §6.3, Spirant Mutation](https://lrc.la.utexas.edu/eieol/iriol/20#grammar_1278); Latin, Greek and Sanskrit forms: standard grammars. The \*-s > \*-h step: Holmer (1947) “Postvocalic *s* in Insular Celtic” (abstract only).
