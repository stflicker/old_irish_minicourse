## Paradigm

Mutation of the next word: [L] lenition · [N] nasalization · [H] aspiration (h- before a vowel, gemination of a consonant) · [0] none. The letters show how the *next* word is mutated, not how the noun itself changes.

### *ech* ‘horse’ · masc o-stem

| Case | Sg form + effect | Pl form + effect |
|---|---|---|
| Nom | **ech** [0] | **eich** [L] |
| Gen | **eich** [L] | **ech** [N] |
| Prep | **euch / eoch** [L] | **echaib** [0] |
| Acc | **ech** [N] | **echu / euchu / eochu** [H] |
| Voc | **a eich** [L] | **a echu / a euchu / a eochu** [H] |

## One point

### Read the pl from the ending and the mutation together

Two pl forms are distinct by spelling (*echaib*, *echu*); two are not (*eich*, *ech*). For these the mutation of the next word decides:

- **eich** [L]: nom pl or gen sg (the sentence decides which).
- **ech** [N]: gen pl or acc sg (again, the sentence decides).
- **echu** [H]: acc pl, and the voc pl copied from it.

**Quick answers**

- **Where does [H] come from?** From the old acc pl ending \*-ūs: the vowel and the \*-s were lost, the aspiration stayed. Written evidence: after the article *inna* (acc pl) Stifter (§4.7) has *inna mmaccu* ‘the sons’, with the initial doubled. EIEOL (§6.3) lists the acc pl of all genders among the aspirating forms but notes that gemination is written only where two words are joined, so examples after a noun and before an adjective are rare.
- **Why is the voc pl *a echu*, not *a eich*?** Old Irish copies the acc pl into the voc («, Stifter p. 43).
- **Why *echaib* with no [H], although \*-ih also ended in *h*?** In Stifter's tables [H] follows only *long vowel + h* (\*-ūh, \*-āh); short \*-ih leaves nothing. This is a pattern in the tables, not a rule Stifter states.
- **Variants *euchu* / *eochu*:** u-infection again (p. 43, note 1).

## Comparison (optional)

### Historical development: why is the pl a mixture of old and new?

| Case | PIE | Proto-Celtic | Primitive Irish | Old Irish |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nom | \*h₁éḱu̯ōs | \*ekʷoi̯ « | \*exʷī | **eich** [L] |
| Gen | \*h₁éḱu̯ōm | \*ekʷom | \*exʷan | **ech** [N] |
| Prep | \*h₁éḱu̯ōis, \*-obʰos | \*ekʷobis « | \*exʷaβih | **echaib** [0] |
| Acc | \*h₁éḱu̯ons | \*ekʷūs | \*exʷūh | **echu** [H] |
| Voc | \*h₁éḱu̯ōs | \*ekʷoi̯ « | \*exʷī | **a echu** [H] « |

Forms from Stifter p. 43. « = replaced by analogy, not a regular sound change.

1. **Nom pl («).** PIE \*-ōs was replaced by \*-oi, the ending of the pronouns (Latin *equī*, Greek *híppoi*; Sanskrit *áśvāḥ* keeps \*-ōs). In Primitive Irish \*-oi̯ and the gen sg \*-ī merged as \*-ī: hence *eich* twice.
2. **Acc pl (regular).** \*-ons > \*-ūs: the nasal fell before \*-s and the vowel was lengthened (Latin *equōs* likewise; Sanskrit *áśvān* keeps the *n*). Then \*-s > \*-h, and the final syllable was lost, leaving [H].
3. **Gen pl (regular).** \*-ōm shortened to \*-om, so it is identical with the acc sg: both [N].
4. **Prep and voc pl («).** \*-obis (from the PIE instrumental pl \*-obʰos) replaced \*-ōis in the prep. The voc pl takes over the acc form instead of the regular \*-ī.

**Summary.** Regular sound change: 2, 3. Analogy («): nom, prep and voc pl. Open: why only a long vowel + \*-s leaves [H].

*Sources:* Stifter (2006) §6.4, p. 43, §4.7; [EIEOL Grammar §6.3, Spirant Mutation](https://lrc.la.utexas.edu/eieol/iriol/20#grammar_1278); Latin, Greek and Sanskrit forms: standard grammars. The \*-s > \*-h step: Holmer (1947) “Postvocalic *s* in Insular Celtic”.
