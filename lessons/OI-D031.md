## One point

### The preverb is the first particle

English and German have a few compound verbs with a single preverb; in Old Irish compound verbs make up the greatest part of the verbal system, and up to four preverbs can stand before the root (we only look at one). Because the preverb is the first particle in the verbal form, the whole verb is in dependent position: compound verbs have conj endings only (Stifter §15.1). The preverb comes before the stress and is separated by the raised dot. Some preverbs have two sources and look alike when unstressed: *do·* can be *to-* or *di-*, *ad·* can be *ad-* or *aith-*, *as·* can be *ess-* or *oss-*.

**Quick answers**

- **How are dictionary forms written?** The underlying preverbs are separated by hyphens from one another and from the root without its stem vowel: *do·scara* = to-scar-; a complex example is *do·ro·ósci* = di-ro-od-scoch-.
- **How do I find the meaning?** Learn each compound as a word; the sum of the parts is usually not enough.
- **Do the simple verb and the compound belong to the same class?** Usually yes: W1 *scaraid* → W1 *do·scara*; W2 *rímid* → W2 *ad·rími*.
