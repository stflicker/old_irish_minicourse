Attic Greek forms. Latin, Greek and Sanskrit forms are the standard paradigms; the Latin ablative and Sanskrit instrumental are separate cases.

## One point

### The same ending, kept differently

All four languages continue the PIE feminine stem in *-eh₂*. Greek and Latin keep a long vowel in most forms. Irish lost all final vowels and kept their trace in the mutation of the next word and in the palatalization of the consonant before the ending (*tuaithe*). Like the o-stems, the Irish prepositional merges several PIE cases into one.

**Quick answers**

- **Is the Irish genitive plural like the Greek?** Yes: both continue \*-om (Greek lengthened it, *khōrôn*; Irish shortened it to \*-om > [N]).
- **Which Irish plural forms are replacements?** The genitive and prepositional plural (Day 16, «).
- **How does Irish differ from Latin?** Latin has an ablative; Irish has one prepositional case.

### Historical development: what is inherited?

| Case | Irish development | Classification |
|---|---|---|
| Nominative singular | \*-eh₂ > \*-ā > — [L] | regular |
| Genitive singular | \*-eh₂s > \*-ās > [H] | regular |
| Prepositional singular | \*-eh₂ei, -eh₂i → \*-ai | replaced « |
| Accusative singular | \*-eh₂m > \*-am > [N] | regular |
| Genitive plural | \*-eh₂om → \*-om | replaced « |
| Prepositional plural | \*-eh₂bʰis → \*-abis | replaced « |

Based on Stifter’s tables on pp. 59–61.

**Summary.** The singular is mostly regular; the genitive and prepositional plural and the prepositional singular are replacements. The comparison with Latin, Greek and Sanskrit is textbook comparative grammar.
