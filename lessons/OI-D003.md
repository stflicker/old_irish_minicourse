## Paradigm
| Case | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| Nominative | **fer** | **fir** |
| Genitive | **fir** | **fer** |
| Prepositional | **fiur** | **feraib** |
| Accusative | **fer** | **firu** |
| Vocative | **a fir** | **a firu** |

*Fer* is a masculine *o*-stem meaning ‘man’; the table gives forms without the article. Compare it mentally with yesterday's *ech*.
## One point
The *fer/fir* alternation illustrates **raising** of a short stressed root vowel: *e* appears as **i** in certain inflectional environments (genitive singular, nominative plural, accusative plural, etc.). The change is **conditioned, not an arbitrary spelling variant**.

Stifter distinguishes abstract **{R}** (root-vowel raising) from superscript **L** (lenition of the next word). They describe entirely different things. Raising is not universal: compare *ech*, whose *e* generally remains *e*.

Read the paradigm twice: first by cases across the singular; then find *fer* and *fir* in the plural.

*Source:* Stifter (2006), §6.5 (illustration of masculine *o*-stems; compare McCone 1996 on relative chronology).

## Comparison (optional)

### 1. An inherited stem class; a new-looking vowel alternation

Old Irish *fer* ‘man’ belongs to the PIE **thematic (*o*-stem) class**; compare Latin *vir*. The conspicuous Old Irish **fer : fir : fiur** opposition, however, should **not** be equated with the inherited PIE **e-grade/o-grade ablaut**. It developed largely through **conditioning by vowels of historically present endings**.

| Form | Earlier morphological environment | Old Irish diagnostic |
|---|---|---|
| *fer* (nom. sg.) | thematic nominative ***-os*** | unraised **e** |
| *fir* (gen. sg.) | Celtic genitive ***-ī*** | palatalized stem-final consonant, raised **i** |
| *fiur* (prep. sg.) | earlier rounded vowel in the oblique ending | **u-infection** alongside rising/palatal effects |
| *fir* (nom. pl.) | plural ending ancestral to ***-oi*** | same visible form as genitive singular |

These are **schematic environments**, not a word-by-word claim that every intermediate form of *fer* is attested.

### 2. Sound change becomes inflection

An older vowel in a following syllable influenced the stressed root; that vowel was then weakened or lost. The **effect remained in the root and/or final consonant** and became usable as a **case marker**. This is a type of **morphologization**: a conditioned phonetic alternation becomes part of how grammatical categories are learned.

### 3. One important innovation in the genitive

Celtic *o*-stem **genitive singular *-ī*** differs from the usually reconstructed PIE thematic genitive ***-osyo***. Latin also uses *-ī* (e.g. *virī*), but their apparent agreement should **not automatically be treated as proof of one Proto-Italo-Celtic innovation**: the comparative history remains debated.

**References:** Stifter (2006), §6.5; Thurneysen on affection/raising and *u*-infection; [Cambridge, “Italo-Celtic,” on the genitive *-ī*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/indoeuropean-language-family/italoceltic/B4955FB7B7A6803DDD727795EA88C065).
