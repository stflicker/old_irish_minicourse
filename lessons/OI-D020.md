## Paradigm

**Following-word effects:** [L] lenition; [N] nasalization; [H] historical aspiration/gemination; [0] no regular L/N/H effect prescribed here. The marker belongs to the form before it and describes the next closely connected word, not a suffix. Effects need not be visible in spelling; phonological and syntactic restrictions still apply. Prepositional = the case often called dative. The vocative particle a itself lenites the noun; a marker at the end of the vocative phrase describes what follows the noun.

### *ingen* ‘girl, daughter’ · feminine a-stem

| Case | Singular form + effect | Plural form + effect |
|---|---|---|
| Nominative | **ingen** [L] | **ingena** [H] |
| Genitive | **ingine** [H] | **ingen** [N] |
| Prepositional | **ingin** [L] | **ingenaib** [0] |
| Accusative | **ingin** [N] | **ingena** [H] |
| Vocative | **a ingen** [L] | **a ingena** [H] |


### *becc* ‘small’ · adjective, feminine a-stem forms

| Case | Singular form + effect | Plural form + effect |
|---|---|---|
| Nominative | **becc** [L] | **becca** [H] |
| Genitive | **bicce** [H] | **becc** [N] |
| Prepositional | **bicc** [L] | **beccaib** [0] |
| Accusative | **bicc** [N] | **becca** [H] |
| Vocative | **becc** [L] | **becca** [H] |

The adjective’s initial mutation comes from the preceding noun. The markers here describe what this adjective form can do to a following closely connected word.


### Feminine agreement in context

| Case / number | Phrase | Effect to notice |
|---|---|---|
| Nominative singular | **ind ingen becc** | ingen [L] → becc |
| Genitive singular | **inna ingine bicce** | ingine [H] → bicce |
| Prepositional singular | **dond ingin bicc** | ingin [L] → bicc |
| Accusative singular | **in n-ingin mbicc** | ingin [N] → mbicc |
| Nominative / accusative plural | **inna ingena becca** | ingena [H] → becca |

Case agreement selects becc/bicc/bicce/becca. The preceding noun separately controls initial mutation; not all such changes are written.

## One point

### Read the forms: function → change

**ind ingen becc** ‘the little girl’ versus **inna ingine bicce** ‘of the little girl’: article, noun and adjective all reflect the genitive.

An adjective of the **o/a-class** takes **a-stem endings after a feminine noun**, even if its dictionary citation form is shown with a masculine example elsewhere. The **gender of the noun**, not the English word ‘little’, determines which adjective set is appropriate.

Notice the **genitive singular** *bicce*: both *ingen → ingine* and *becc → bicce* have changed. The **accusative** *mbicc* also carries visible nasalization in the noun phrase.

Try covering the right column and building the *genitive singular* and *nominative plural* on your own.

*Source:* Stifter (2006), §11.4.

## Comparison (optional)

### 1. PIE gender agreement and feminine morphology

In much of the Indo-European family, an adjectival lexical stem appears with **masculine/neuter thematic ***-o-*** inflection but **feminine ***-eh₂-*** inflection**. The Old Irish adjective **becc** ‘small’ belongs to a corresponding **o/a-type**, so in *ingen becc* the adjective is **feminine**, despite *becc* also being a masculine dictionary form.

### 2. Inheritance and remodeling of the phrase

| Inflectional property | Older structural origin | Old Irish realization |
|---|---|---|
| adjective agrees in gender | Indo-European agreement | feminine with **ingen** |
| adjective agrees in case | Indo-European case morphology | **becc / bicce / bicc** according to case |
| article and adjective show effects at their boundaries | Celtic/Irish grammaticalized sandhi | e.g. **mbicc** with accusative nasalization |

Compare Latin **bonus** (masc.) and **bona** (fem.) only for **stem-class agreement**, not as lexical cognates of *becc*.

### 3. An innovation in where agreement becomes visible

The case and gender information is **distributed across the article, noun and adjective**, rather than always represented by transparent endings on all three. A historical nominal suffix can be **lost as a vowel** while its phonological effects remain **grammatically meaningful**. This change in *exponence* is more informative than simply saying that Old Irish “lost the PIE endings.”
