## Paradigm
| Person | Absolute | Conjunct after **ní** |
|---|---|---|
| 1st singular | **leiciu** (also **leicim(m)**) | **ní·leiciu** / **ní·leicim(m)** |
| 2nd singular | **leici** | **ní·leici** |
| 3rd singular | **leicid** | **ní·leici** |
| 1st plural | **leicmi** | **ní·leicem** |
| 2nd plural | **leicthe** | **ní·leicid** |
| 3rd plural | **leicit** | **ní·leicet** |

The W2 class is traditionally called the **i-verbs**: note the third singular **conjunct -i**, not W1 **-a**.
## One point
The W2a model is **leicid** ‘leaves, permits’ (Stifter §12.1). Its third singular **leicid : ní·leici** makes a perfect contrast with yesterday's W1 **caraid : ní·cara**. The **stem-final consonant is normally palatalized** throughout W2 paradigms.

The first singular alternates between historically older **-iu** and spreading **-im(m)**. Today you need only **leicid**, **ní·leici**, and the key plural **leicit : ní·leicet**. You can treat the other rows as recognition rather than memorization.

**Source:** Stifter (2006), §12.1, Illustration 12.1; cf. Thurneysen, *A Grammar of Old Irish*, §336–337.

## Comparison (optional)

### 1. W2a belongs to the old Indo-European derived-verb system

Old Irish **W2** verbs have a third-singular **conjunct form in *-i***, unlike W1's *-a*. A central source for many **W2a** stems is a **denominative** or related derived verb formation, historically associated with an Indo-European ***-eyé- / -i̯e-*** family of suffixes and developed in Celtic into an **ī-type verbal stem**.

| Level | Illustration | Historical function |
|---|---|---|
| PIE | verbal derivatives in an ***-eyé-*** type | form a verb from a nominal/adjectival or verbal base |
| Proto-Celtic | **ī-type** derived present stems | class formation and remodeling |
| Old Irish W2a | **leicid : ní·leici** | absolute/conjunct inflection of a weak verb |
| Old Irish W1 | **caraid : ní·cara** | contrast: conjunct 3sg **-a** |

The historical **stem-building suffix** and the **finite person ending** are **two different morphological layers**.

### 2. Why *-i* is not just a lost PIE final vowel

PIE derivational suffixes, Celtic vowel developments, and the later Irish **absolute/conjunct split** all contribute to the surface form. W2 **-i** is best learned as the **synchronic reflex of a class-defining stem formation**, not as a directly inherited PIE “third-person ending *-i*.”

### 3. Innovation in category, not merely form

The W1/W2 division represents the **reorganization of older derivational types into productive Irish conjugational classes**. Individual W2a verbs can have complex lexical histories, so do not reconstruct each form by a single *-eyé-* equation without evidence.
