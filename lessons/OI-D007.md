Attic Greek forms. Latin, Greek and Sanskrit forms are the standard paradigms. The dat pl of Latin and Greek continues the old instrumental/locative pl; Sanskrit has a separate dat in *-ebhyaḥ* (instrumental *áśvaiḥ* < \*-ōis).

## One point

### Same stem, different choices

All four languages build the noun from a root plus the **thematic vowel** \*-o- plus an ending (hence “o-stem”: Latin *equ-o-*, Greek *hipp-o-*, Irish *ech* < \*ekʷ-o-). They differ in which endings they kept.

- **Gen sg:** Greek and Sanskrit keep \*-osi̯o; Latin and Celtic have \*-ī.
- **Nom pl:** Greek and Latin have pronominal \*-oi; Sanskrit has \*-ōs.
- **Acc pl:** \*-ons survives only in Sanskrit; Latin, Greek (Attic) and Celtic lost the nasal and lengthened the vowel.
- **Gen pl:** Greek and Celtic keep \*-ōm; Latin *-ōrum* and Sanskrit *-ānām* are extended.

**Quick answers**

- **Is Irish “closer” to Latin?** Only on the gen sg, and that ending arose separately in each branch (Weiss). Italo-Celtic as a subgroup remains debated.
- **Why is the Irish prep so different?** Celtic merged PIE dat, instrumental and ablative (Day 1); Latin has dat \*-ōi (*equō*) and a separate ablative.
- **Which Irish forms are not simple reflexes?** The « forms of Stifter's chains: gen sg, nom, prep and voc pl (see the table).

## Comparison (optional)

### Historical development: Irish o-stem endings, inherited or replaced?

| Case | Irish development | Classification |
|---|---|---|
| Nom. sg | \*-os > \*-ah > — | regular |
| Gen. sg | \*-osi̯o → \*-ī | replaced « |
| Prep sg | \*-ōi, \*-oh₁, \*-ōd → \*-ū | regular merger |
| Acc. sg | \*-om > \*-an > [N] | regular |
| Voc. sg | \*-e | regular |
| Nom. pl | \*-ōs → \*-oi → \*-ī | replaced « |
| Gen. pl | \*-ōm > \*-om > \*-an > [N] | regular |
| Prep pl | \*-ōis → \*-obis > \*-aβih | replaced « |
| Acc. pl | \*-ons > \*-ūs > \*-ūh > [H] | regular |
| Voc. pl | \*-ōs → \*-oi → \*-ī, then replaced by the acc pl | replaced « |

Chains from Stifter p. 43 (Days 1–2). « = replaced by analogy.

1. **What survives from the PIE ending:** vowel quality (palatal, rounded) and the mutation of the next word. The case is shown in the neighbouring word rather than in the ending.
2. **What Irish shares:** with Latin the gen \*-ī; with Greek and Latin the nom \*-oi; with Greek the gen pl \*-ōm.
3. **What is Irish alone:** the merger of three PIE cases in one prep case, and the loss of every final syllable.

**Summary.** Six of the ten forms are regular developments (counting the prep sg merger); four are replacements («). The comparison with Latin, Greek and Sanskrit is textbook comparative grammar.

*Sources:* Stifter (2006) §6.4, p. 43; Weiss, “Italo-Celtic”, in *The Indo-European Language Family*; Latin, Greek and Sanskrit paradigms: standard grammars (e.g. Fortson, *Indo-European Language and Culture*).
