## One point

### Same stem, different choices

All five languages build the noun from a root plus the **thematic vowel** \*-o- plus an ending (hence “o-stem”: Latin *equ-o-*, Greek *hipp-o-*, Irish *ech* < \*ekʷ-o-). They differ in which endings they kept.

- **Genitive singular:** Greek and Sanskrit keep \*-osi̯o; Latin and Celtic have \*-ī.
- **Nominative plural:** Greek and Latin have pronominal \*-oi; Sanskrit has \*-ōs.
- **Accusative plural:** \*-ons survives only in Sanskrit; Latin, Greek (Attic) and Celtic lost the nasal and lengthened the vowel.
- **Genitive plural:** Greek and Celtic keep \*-ōm; Latin *-ōrum* and Sanskrit *-ānām* are extended.

**Quick answers**

- **Is Irish “closer” to Latin?** Only on the genitive singular, and that ending arose separately in each branch (Weiss). Italo-Celtic as a subgroup remains debated.
- **Why is the Irish dative so different?** Celtic merged PIE dative, instrumental and ablative (Day 1); Latin has dative \*-ōi (*equō*) and a separate ablative.
- **Which Irish cells are not simple reflexes?** The «-cells of Stifter's chains: genitive singular, nominative, dative and vocative plural (see the card).

Chains from Stifter p. 43 (Days 1–2). « = replaced by analogy.

1. **What survives from the PIE ending:** vowel quality (palatal, rounded) and the mutation of the next word. The case signal moved from the ending to the neighbour.
2. **What Irish shares:** with Latin the genitive \*-ī; with Greek and Latin the nominative \*-oi; with Greek the genitive plural \*-ōm.
3. **What is Irish alone:** the merger of three PIE cases in one dative, and the loss of every final syllable.

**Status.** The classification of the Irish endings follows Stifter's «; the comparison with Latin, Greek and Sanskrit is textbook comparative grammar.
