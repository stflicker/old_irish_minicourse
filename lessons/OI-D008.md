## Paradigm

Mutation of the next word: [L] lenition · [N] nasalization · [H] aspiration (h- before a vowel, gemination of a consonant) · [0] none. The letters show how the *next* word is mutated, not how the article itself changes.

### The article ‘the’ · masc · sg (before b, d, g, p, t, c, m)

| Case | Sg article + effect | Example phrase |
|---|---|---|
| Nom | **in** [0] | **in catt** ‘the cat’ |
| Gen | **in** [L] | **in chaitt** ‘of the cat’ |
| Prep | **-(s)in** [L] | **don chatt** ‘to the cat’ |
| Acc | **in** [N] | **in catt** ‘the cat’ (object) |
| Voc | **a** [L] (particle; no article) | **a chaitt** ‘O cat’ |

The prep article is fused with the preposition (second table). The voc never has an article; the particle *a* lenites the noun.

### Preposition + article

| Preposition | Alone | With the article (sg) |
|---|---|---|
| *do* ‘to, for’ | **do** | **don** |
| *ar* ‘in front of’ | **ar** | **arin** |
| *i* ‘in’ | **i** | **isin** |

## One point

### The article makes the case visible

Nom and acc of *catt* are both **catt**. The article has the same effects as the o-stem noun itself (Stifter §7.1, note 1): [0] in the nom, [N] in the acc, [L] in the gen and prep. So the case can be read in the next word, whether that is the noun or an adjective.

**Quick answers**

- **When do I write the article with an *-s-* (*isin*) and when without (*don*)?** The choice depends on the preposition. If it lenites the next word (*do*, *fo*), the fused form has no *s*: *don chatt*. For all other prepositions (*i*, *as*, *la*) it has an *s*: *isin chatt*. Exceptions: *tre* has the *s*-form; *for* has both; *oc* never has *s*.
- **Why is there an *-s-* at all?** The *s* is old: it is the first sound of the Proto-Celtic article, \*sindos (masc) and \*sinda (fem), which is also why the masc nom is *int* before a vowel. It survives after prepositions that do not lenite. Why lenition of the preposition should decide whether the *s* is kept is not explained by Stifter (§7.1 note 3), and short descriptions of the Old Irish article (for instance Baturin’s) only state the same link. Treat it as a rule to learn with each preposition.
- **Can the article stand in the prep case without a preposition?** Almost never. Stifter knows one line of verse and a few frozen phrases (*in chruth-so* ‘thus’). So in practice the prep article always appears fused with a preposition, as in *don* (*do* + *in*) or *isin* (*i* + *in*), and you will not meet a bare prep *in*.
- **Is *in* always spelled the same?** No: with a vowel or *l, r, n, f, s* the forms change (Day 9).

## Comparison (optional)

### Historical development: where does the article come from?

| Case | Proto-Celtic (Stifter) | Old Irish |
|---|---|---|
| Nom | \*(s)indos | **in** [0] |
| Gen | \*(s)indi | **in** [L] |
| Acc | \*(s)indom | **in** [N] |

1. The article continues a Proto-Celtic dem \*(s)indos with the same endings as an o-stem noun. That is why its effects are those of the o-stem noun.
2. Stifter says it is hard to know whether the *n* of *in* was pal in some cases; he does not mark it.

**Summary.** The effects are the regular ones; the s-forms and the fusion with prepositions are Irish developments.
