## Paradigm

Mutation of the next word: [L] lenition · [N] nasalization · [H] aspiration (h- before a vowel, gemination of a consonant) · [0] none. The letters show how the *next* word is mutated, not how the article itself changes.

### The article ‘the’ · masculine · singular (before b, d, g, p, t, c, m)

| Case | Singular article + effect | Example phrase |
|---|---|---|
| Nominative | **in** [0] | **in catt** ‘the cat’ |
| Genitive | **in** [L] | **in chaitt** ‘of the cat’ |
| Prepositional | **-(s)in** [L] | **don chatt** ‘to the cat’ |
| Accusative | **in** [N] | **in catt** ‘the cat’ (object) |
| Vocative | **a** [L] (particle; no article) | **a chaitt** ‘O cat’ |

The prepositional article is fused with the preposition (second table). The vocative never has an article; the particle *a* lenites the noun.

### Preposition + article

| Preposition | Alone | With the article (sg.) |
|---|---|---|
| *do* ‘to, for’ | **do** | **don** |
| *ar* ‘in front of’ | **ar** | **arin** |
| *i* ‘in’ | **i** | **isin** |

## One point

### The article makes the case visible

Nominative and accusative of *catt* are both **catt**. The article has the same effects as the o-stem noun itself (Stifter §7.1, note 1): [0] in the nominative, [N] in the accusative, [L] in the genitive and prepositional. So the case can be read in the next word, whether that is the noun or an adjective.

**Quick answers**

- **Why does the form with *-s-* exist?** Prepositions that lenite (such as *do*, *fo*) use the forms without s (*don*); the others (such as *i*, *as*) use the forms with s (*isin*). Exceptions: *tre* takes the s-form, *for* both, and *oc* never has the s (§7.1 note 3).
- **Does the article ever stand alone in the prepositional?** Very rarely; in practice it is fused with a preposition. It also fuses with prepositions that take the accusative, with the same rule for s.
- **Is *in* always spelled the same?** No: with a vowel or *l, r, n, f, s* the forms change (Day 9).

## Comparison (optional)

### Historical development: where does the article come from?

| Case | Proto-Celtic (Stifter) | Old Irish |
|---|---|---|
| Nominative | \*(s)indos | **in** [0] |
| Genitive | \*(s)indi | **in** [L] |
| Accusative | \*(s)indom | **in** [N] |

1. The article continues a Proto-Celtic demonstrative \*(s)indos with the same endings as an o-stem noun. That is why its effects are those of the o-stem noun.
2. Stifter says it is hard to know whether the *n* of *in* was palatalized in some cases; he does not mark it.

**Summary.** The effects are the regular ones; the s-forms and the fusion with prepositions are Irish developments. Unresolved: the exact palatalization of the *n*. The prepositional forms in Stifter’s table are garbled in the text layer and are not given here.
