## One point

### One more syllable, a different syncope

The prot form has one more syllable than the deut. Syncope deletes the vowel of every second non-final syllable, and the quality of the lost vowel decides the palatalization of the cluster (front vowel: palatalized; back vowel: non-palatalized). Four derivations (Stifter §15.2):

- *ad·ella* → *·adella* → syncope of e (front) → **·aidlea**
- *ad·rími* → *·adrími* → *d* lost before *r* with compensatory lengthening → *·arími* → syncope of *í* (front) → **·airmi**
- *do·scara* → *·toscara* (o becomes a before a following a) → *·tascara* → syncope of a (back) → **·tascra**
- *do·sluindi* → *·disluindi* → lenition *s* > *h*: *·dihluindi* → syncope of u (back) → *·dihlndai* → voiced consonant next to *h* devoiced (d > t), *h* lost, *n* assimilated to *l*: **·díltai**

A result: in **·díltai** the root-final consonant is non-palatalized throughout, although W2 verbs ideally palatalize it; the opposite can happen in W1 (*for·cenna* has *·foircnea*).

**Quick answers**

- **Is ·díltai the most extreme case?** No: Stifter says there are more extreme alternations (GOI 534–536 has a list).
- **Where do I find both forms?** In Stifter’s vocabulary both the deut and prot 3sg are given for every compound verb.
- **Can an unexpected form appear?** Yes, because of the many complicated developments in Old Irish.
